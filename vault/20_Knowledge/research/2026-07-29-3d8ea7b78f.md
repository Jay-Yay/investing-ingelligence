Space Economy: A New 
Investment Frontier
A multi-faceted market having its 
moment
Space is not one market. It spans multiple applications including 
communications, sensing, compute, navigation, defense, and 
science and exploration. Space also operates across multiple 
orbital planes, each with distinct physics, economics and use 
cases. Demand for capabilities in space is being driven by both 
commercial and government needs, creating multiple growth 
vectors across the value chain. 
Three exponentials are converging to give space its moment.
1. The cost of reaching orbit is collapsing. Space economics are 
driven by a key metric: cost per kilogram to orbit. NASA’s Space 
Shuttle cost roughly USD$54,000/kg. SpaceX recently built a 
partially reusable craft at 5% of that cost, with a fully reusable 
model in development that targets another order-of-magnitude 
cost reduction.1 As launch costs fall, entirely new markets 
become viable, from mega-constellations and space-based 
defense architectures to orbital data centers and potentially 
lunar operations.
Tony Kim
Head of the Global 
Technology Team, 
Fundamental 
Equities

The space economy is no longer a distant or speculative 
theme. We find it is already delivering economic value 
through communications, navigation, Earth observation, 
defense systems, weather data, logistics tracking and 
satellite-enabled connectivity. 
Yet the scale of the opportunity is changing. A convergence 
of supply-side breakthroughs — most importantly falling 
launch costs, reusable rockets, smaller satellites and AI-
enabled autonomy — is meeting powerful demand-side 
drivers from AI, defense, data, connectivity and 
infrastructure. Together, these forces are creating an 
inflection point in the space economy and could represent 
a significant infrastructure investment opportunity of the 
next decade.

2. AI is expanding both the demand for and 
capabilities of space. Historically, the 
complexity of turning satellite data into 
actionable insights limited commercial 
adoption. AI is changing that by transforming 
imagery, signals, weather and tracking data into 
real-time intelligence for industries ranging 
from logistics and insurance to energy and 
defense.2 At the same time, satellite networks 
provide the connectivity needed to extend AI to 
users, devices, sensors and machines globally, 
while AI-enabled satellites increasingly process 
data onboard, coordinate autonomously and 
act at the edge. Looking further ahead, space 
could become a new frontier for AI 
infrastructure as rising compute demand 
collides with terrestrial power constraints.
3. Government and defense spending are 
accelerating into space. The U.S., its allies and 
strategic competitors increasingly view space 
as a core national security domain.3 The U.S. 
Space Force funding request is set to rise 
roughly 78% from 2026 to 2027, increasing 
its share of total defense spending from 
approximately 3% to 5%,4 alongside growing 
investment in launch capabilities, satellite 
constellations and space-based defense 
systems.

stack. Launch is the foundation. Satellites and 
payloads sit above it. Ground infrastructure 
connects orbit to Earth. Applications and services 
sit on top.

investment

major potential growth engine for space. Near-

how central space assets are to resilient 
communications, real-time intelligence, precision 
targeting and faster decision-making.5

and imagery into missile warning and tracking, 
AI-enabled battle management, in-orbit servicing, 
autonomous satellite repositioning, cyber and

Deep space exploration and the moon. The moon 
is back on the agenda as humanity’s first non-
terrestrial economic and strategic frontier. NASA's 
Artemis II mission, launched in April 2026, was the 
first crewed mission beyond low earth orbit in over 
50 years. A crewed lunar surface mission is 
planned for 2028. Beneath these government-led 
programs, a commercial ecosystem of private 
companies is increasingly providing landers, rovers, 
communications relays and surface power systems.
The moon offers unique economic and logistical 
advantages: Water ice at the lunar South Pole 
could support propellant and life-support 
production, while lunar materials may eventually 
be processed onsite. Its low gravity and lack of 
atmosphere also make it a potential staging 
ground for Mars and deeper-space missions, 
and could one day enable lower-energy cargo 
launches via electromagnetic mass drivers.
The next investing frontier
Space is creating multiple ways to invest across 
the stack, from traditional defense primes to 
newer entrants and specialist suppliers. But the 
winners will not all look the same. Some will be 
driven by government and defense budgets; 
others by AI-led commercial demand for data, 
connectivity and compute. We believe the most 
attractive opportunities may sit where those two 
engines overlap. 
This is also a physically constrained system. Space 
is hard to build, difficult to scale, technically 
complex and capital intensive — particularly in 
areas like launch, manufacturing and mission-
critical infrastructure. These bottlenecks limit 
supply, slow competition and create areas where 
scarcity, reliability, technical capability and 
customer trust can support durable returns. 
Space is no longer a speculative theme, but a 
strategic investment ecosystem. As the system 
scales, demand should broaden across defense, 
space infrastructure, AI and applications. Yet 
value capture will be uneven, in our view, making 
this an active investment opportunity. The focus 
should be on where bottlenecks are real, moats are 
durable, and on those companies positioned to 
capitalize on supply/demand dynamics as the 
space economy builds out.

From an economic perspective, we see this shift

Data centers in space. Building AI data centers on 
Earth is becoming increasingly difficult. Each new 
data center must secure grid interconnection in 
queues that are already backlogged, source gas 
turbines with delivery timelines stretching into 
2030, and navigate local permitting processes 
facing growing community resistance. We estimate 
a 1GW AI data center costs roughly USD$40 billion-
USD$50 billion to build on Earth.6
In orbit, those constraints invert. Solar panels in 
space receive cleaner, more continuous sunlight 
by avoiding atmospheric losses, clouds, weather 
and much of the day-night cycle. This allows them 
to collect roughly 5x more solar energy than those 
on Earth.7 At the same time, the vacuum of space 
provides natural cooling, eliminating one of the 
largest costs of running a data center. There is no 
grid to connect to, no water to source, and no 
neighbors to object. Computing hardware and 
power generation can sit side by side in orbit. 
On Earth, each incremental gigawatt gets harder. 
In orbit, the opposite is true — scale improves 
the economics.8
While certain engineering challenges remain 
to be solved, we believe all are tractable. In 
January 2026, SpaceX filed an FCC application for 
an orbital data center constellation of up to one 
million satellites, operating between 500- and 
2,000-kilometers altitude, and other companies 
are evaluating similar concepts.9 In our view, 
orbital space centers could become the only way 
to scale computing beyond 100GW of new global 
data center capacity by 2030, particularly if they 
become cheaper to build and operate than their 
Earth-based counterparts.

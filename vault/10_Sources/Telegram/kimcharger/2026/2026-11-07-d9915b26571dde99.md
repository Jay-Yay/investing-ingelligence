### Apollo's $75 Billion Chip Leasing Push Takes Shape, Far Outpacing All Neoclouds — BigGo Finance

Apollo's $75 Billion Chip Leasing Push Takes Shape, Far Outpacing All Neoclouds
Private capital giant Apollo Global Management is pushing into the AI compute leasing market with two chip financing deals totaling about $75 billion. The scale of these two transactions clearly exceeds the total chip financing of all neoclouds combined, making Apollo one of the institutions holding the most AI chip assets outside the hyperscalers.
According to The Information, SpaceX has announced it will lease about $40 billion worth of Nvidia Vera Rubin chips, with Apollo purchasing the chips through a subsidiary and subleasing them to SpaceX. The other deal, announced in June this year, saw Apollo team up with Broadcom and Blackstone to arrange $35 billion in financing to buy Google tensor processing units (TPUs) and lease them to AI startup Anthropic. Combined, Apollo's footprint in AI chip financing has opened up a clear gap over rivals.
Looking at the historical scale of chip-backed financing, CoreWeave has raised about $27 billion cumulatively since 2023, ranking first among neoclouds; it is followed by Valor ($5.4 billion), Nscale ($4.9 billion), IREN ($3.9 billion), Lambda ($1.5 billion), Crusoe ($1.1 billion), Nebius ($775 million) and Sharon AI ($356 million). Apollo's single $35 billion deal alone exceeds the combined total of all such players except CoreWeave.
Note: The above statistics cover only financing collateralized by GPUs and TPUs, and exclude convertible bonds and credit facilities issued by neoclouds to fund operations.
Deal structure follows SPV model; Apollo does not build data centers
The financing architecture Apollo uses derives from the special purpose vehicle (SPV) model pioneered by CoreWeave and Blackstone in 2023. Under the arrangement, a neocloud and a financial partner jointly set up an SPV, which issues debt to buy chips, with rental payments from end users used to service the debt. Apollo's operating logic is the same, but on a significantly larger scale.
Unlike most neoclouds, Apollo has not invested additional capital to build data centers to house the chips. Its business model is more focused: buy chips, lease them to end users, and earn transaction arrangement fees and fund management fees. Hyperscalers do not need the SPV structure and can complete purchases directly with free cash flow, equity issuance or traditional corporate bonds — the essential difference between Apollo and giants such as Amazon, Microsoft and Google.
The specific terms of the $40 billion SpaceX deal are also gradually emerging. According to Bloomberg, Goldman Sachs and Apollo are approaching Wall Street investors to distribute part of the financing, with a formal syndication process about to begin. The deal involves an SPV managed by Apollo-backed Atlas SP buying Nvidia chips and issuing debt, split into multiple tranches, including an $8.3 billion senior secured loan and multiple tranches of bonds issued to asset managers. Goldman is leading the loan portion, Apollo is the main investor in the bonds, and Pacific Investment Management Co. (PIMCO) is also participating.
Documents show that the debt, together with a $7 billion lease prepayment, will be used to pay for about $47.3 billion of GPU equipment — Nvidia's latest Rubin chips, expected to be delivered in the first half of 2027. SpaceX will lease the chips under a five-year agreement but is not providing residual value guarantees or other commitments commonly made by tech companies to obtain financing. The documents stress that SpaceX's rental payment obligation is absolute and cannot be suspended due to equipment condition, damage or force majeure.
Insurance capital underpins the bet, extending private credit logic to compute assets
Apollo's chip gamble is closely tied to the capital structure transformation of this veteran private equity firm over the past decade. Apollo, co-founded by billionaire Leon Black more than 36 years ago, made its name in leveraged buyouts, including the nearly $30 billion acquisition of casino giant Harrah's Entertainment in 2008. Over the past decade, the firm has absorbed insurance company capital on a large scale. Insurance funds favor assets with yields above investment-grade bonds but credit ratings that remain investment grade, and chip financing deals fit that need precisely.
Chip financing is a brand-new business direction for Apollo, but the underlying logic is consistent with traditional private credit: provide structured financing collateralized by assets and earn a stable spread. As AI compute demand continues to swell, Apollo is pushing this model to a new order of magnitude.
Market impact: financialization of compute assets accelerates, risk pricing faces a test
If both deals close smoothly, Apollo will rank behind only hyperscalers such as Amazon, Microsoft and Google in the scale of AI chips it holds, becoming the non-hyperscale player with the deepest chip reserves globally. That means the competitive landscape for AI infrastructure is changing: chips are no longer just a capital expenditure item for tech companies, but also a financial asset that private credit institutions can package and distribute.
For investors, the appeal of such deals lies in a relatively clear cash flow structure — rental payments from end users map directly to debt service. But the risks are equally concentrated: chip delivery cycles are long, technology iterates quickly, and rental income depends heavily on the creditworthiness of a single lessee. The arrangement in the SpaceX deal — no residual value guarantee and absolute rental obligations — is precisely designed to give bond investors a clearer credit transmission path during periods of market volatility.
It is worth noting that the above statistics do not include another $63 billion deal arranged jointly by Citigroup, Bank of America and Blackstone to finance Anthropic's purchase of Broadcom chips, which was excluded from the comparison because the parties did not disclose the actual holder of the chips. If such deals are included, the total volume of AI chip-related debt financing would swell further, and its impact on concentration in the private credit market will be a focus for regulators and rating agencies going forward.

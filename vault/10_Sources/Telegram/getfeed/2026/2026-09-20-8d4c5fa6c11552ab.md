### DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression | alphaXiv

(https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.19969)

Submitted 17 Sept 2026
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression
#1 on Trending
Abstract
The widespread adoption of long-horizon agents has made model workloads increasingly input-heavy. Although prior work has substantially reduced the cost of long-context computation, prefill remains computationally expensive, and large KV caches continue to strain HBM and SSD capacity and data-transfer bandwidth. Together, these compute, storage, and bandwidth demands constitute the primary bottleneck to further lowering deployment costs. To address this challenge, we introduce DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a multimodal Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with 552B backbone parameters and support for contexts of up to one million tokens. With its Causal Encoder-Decoder (CED) architecture, the model activates 16B parameters per token during decode but only 8B parameters during prefill, substantially improving cost efficiency for agentic workloads. To push the limits of KV cache compression, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash combines cross-layer KV cache reuse in Compressed Sparse Attention 2 (CSA2) with FP4 KV caching. These designs reduce its global KV cache footprint (always in HBM) to 890 bytes per token, roughly 1/4 of the corresponding footprint of DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Further, through a dedicated deployment optimization known as SWA Bounded Replay, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reduces its persistent KV cache footprint (always on SSD or in host memory) to roughly 1/8 of that of DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Despite its much smaller KV cache footprint, the model delivers substantially better performance than the baseline. In addition, we streamline the DeepSeek-V4 architecture and introduce several efficient architectural extensions. We pretrain DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on a multimodal corpus comprising 45T tokens and conduct comprehensive post-training, yielding strong performance across diverse text-based and multimodal agentic scenarios. Model checkpoints are available at this https URL.
AI Overview
Imagine an AI assistant that helps you work through a complex coding project. Each time it calls a tool or reads a file, that action gets added to a growing conversation it must reread before responding again. After dozens of tool calls, the history can reach hundreds of thousands of words. Processing that history requires fast computation, but it also requires storing and moving a large block of numerical state—called a key-value (KV) cache—that records what the model has "seen." At long enough horizons, moving and storing that state becomes the real bottleneck, not just the arithmetic. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash tackles this by reusing long-range memory across many layers, storing it in fewer bits, and rebuilding a small amount of local state on demand rather than persisting it—while keeping performance competitive with much larger models. The result is a global cache footprint of 890 bytes per token in fast GPU memory, roughly one quarter that of its predecessor, and a persistent prefix-reuse footprint roughly one eighth as large, with stronger results on agentic coding tasks.
Where the cost moved
Running a language model on a long conversation involves three distinct expenses. First is prefill: processing every new or uncached input token to build the initial state. Second is live storage in the GPU's fast on-chip memory (HBM): the KV cache records a compact representation of each token that later tokens can attend to. Third is persistent storage in slower host memory or on SSDs: when a conversation prefix has been seen before, that prefix cache avoids reprocessing it.
Local sliding-window attention—where each token looks only at its nearest neighbors—has bounded storage regardless of total context length. The expensive part is global attention, where the model looks back across the entire history. That global KV state grows with context, dominates HBM at long sequences, and must be written to and read from persistent storage for multi-turn agents. DeepSeek-V4 had already reduced attention computation through sparsity, but every layer still produced its own global KV state, and the sliding-window state kept on SSDs required a prohibitively long recomputation pass to exactly rebuild when missing.
Four levers applied together
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash addresses those costs through four coordinated changes.
Cheaper prefill. The 40-layer model is divided into a 20-layer causal encoder and a 20-layer decoder. Most prompt tokens are processed only by the encoder. The decoder's global KV state is projected from the encoder's final hidden representations using learned layer-specific weights, rather than being recomputed from each decoder layer's own activations. This nearly halves prefill computation while maintaining baseline-comparable performance. The practical effect is that the model activates only 8B parameters per token during prefill and 16B during decode—a favorable profile for agents that read far more than they write.
Shared global memory across layers. Traditional transformers keep a separate KV cache for every layer. Compressed Sparse Attention 2 (CSA2) assigns each layer one of three roles: a Full layer creates global KV, chooses sparse Top-K context positions, and builds a candidate pool; a Reindex layer borrows that same global KV but runs its own lightweight scorer to pick fresh positions; and a Reuse layer borrows both the global KV and the previously chosen positions, skipping the scoring step entirely. All three still form their own query and local sliding-window state. Sharing global KV and position scores across many layers avoids duplicating the largest part of the cache, while decoupling KV sharing from position-index sharing lets deeper layers adapt their sparse selections without a fresh read from the full context.
Lower-precision storage. The main global KV is stored in FP4 format—a four-bit number—during training, via quantization-aware training (QAT), meaning the model learns to tolerate the reduced precision rather than having it applied after the fact. The authors note that FP4 reduces storage rather than accelerating matrix multiplication; cached values are dequantized before attention, preserving hardware compatibility. The sliding-window KV remains in FP8 due to its measured sensitivity to further compression.
Approximate local reconstruction. Sliding-window attention lets each token attend only to its most recent neighbors—say, the last 128 tokens. Because that window shifts with every new turn, storing it permanently on SSDs is costly and rarely useful: the state is only needed for seconds or minutes within an active session. Exact reconstruction would require replaying 128 × 20 = 2,560 tokens through all decoder layers. SWA Bounded Replay instead replays only the most recent 128 tokens through each layer's sliding window, truncating attention to that segment. The authors report that this approximate reconstruction barely compromises response quality, though the paper does not provide a dedicated ablation table isolating this step.
The combined effect: removing persistent sliding-window storage nearly halves the persistent cache, and the remaining global KV is compressed to one quarter of V4's footprint through the architectural and precision changes.
Evidence for the storage–capability trade-off
The central benchmark for evaluating agentic capability is DeepSWE v1.1, a software-engineering task set where the score ("Resolved") is the fraction of repository-level tasks that the model's agent successfully completes, verified automatically. Under Max reasoning effort (the highest effort tier), the mini-SWE harness, a 1M-token context window, temperature 1.0, and top-p 0.95, V4.1-Flash resolves 74.2% of tasks, versus 54.4% for V4-Flash and 74.0% for Opus-5.
That capability result cannot be attributed to cache compression alone. V4.1-Flash is a different backbone—552B total parameters, 45T-token multimodal pretraining, and a substantially larger and more diverse reinforcement-learning environment compared with V4-Flash. The fair reading is that the complete system, including architectural compression, achieves these results; isolating any single contribution would require controlled ablations that the paper does not provide.
The paper does show that the decode FLOPs model—precision-weighted arithmetic operations per generated token—stays nearly constant across context lengths, as Figure 2 makes visible. However, that is a compute model rather than a wall-clock measurement; no central V4.1-versus-V4 end-to-end throughput table is reported.
The robustness caveat the authors themselves name: sparse-selection errors and approximate state reconstruction may cause capability degradation in untested boundary cases, particularly on long-context retrieval and cache-resumption edge cases.
Smaller state, capable agent
The paper's core demonstration is that aggressive KV cache compression—applied jointly to architecture, numeric precision, and deployment strategy—does not require trading away capability. By sharing long-range context across layers, storing it in four-bit precision, and rebuilding local state from a short replay window, V4.1-Flash reaches a persistent cache footprint that makes million-token multi-turn agents substantially cheaper to host, while the same model resolves a larger fraction of real software-engineering tasks than its predecessor. The mechanism connecting those outcomes is a coherent system design, and the evidence—storage ratios, weighted-FLOPs curves, and a broad benchmark sweep—supports it within the limits of what the paper measures. Model weights are available at the Hugging Face repository.

### Why Old Electronics Could Be Worth 100 Times More Than Actual Gold Mines - Futura-Sciences

(https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/why-old-electronics-could-be-worth-100-times-more-than-actual-gold-mines_38510/)

Striking Gold: Where You Least Expect It
It may sound far-fetched, but researchers at ETH Zurich (Swiss Federal Institute of Technology) are having a real gouda time with this: they’ve crafted a lab-only sponge out of whey protein—the leftover liquid from cheese production—that has a remarkable talent for soaking up dissolved gold from discarded electronics. To give you an idea, twenty used motherboards gave up enough gold for a 22-carat nugget. Not too shabby for yesterday’s tech!
But before you go panning through your junk drawer in a chef’s hat, a quick warning: the entire process is strictly experimental, and even the researchers advise,
“Do not try this at home!”
Electronics: A New Gold Rush, But Not Without Peril
Out-of-service electronics—everything from TVs to smartphones and gaming consoles—contain considerable amounts of gold. The problem? DIY enthusiasts often resort to strong acids, with little to no protective equipment, risking serious health and environmental consequences. That’s where the Swiss team’s method stands out.
Instead of just reaching for dangerous chemicals, their novel step comes after the initial, still-necessary acid bath: the gold-capturing miracle sponge. Crafted from whey—a by-product that the dairy industry produces by the tankerload—this protein, once heated in acidic conditions, denatures and forms minuscule strands called nanofibrils. These build into a very porous gel, which, once dried, becomes a sponge adept at selectively grabbing gold ions, faring far better than when faced with other, less valuable metals.
The full procedure is very much a multi-step laboratory affair. As the team makes clear (underscored by the sudden flood of DIY requests!), the process starts with twenty used motherboards, which are crushed and dropped into an acid bath to dissolve the metals and release ions into solution. The protein sponge then plunges in, selectively capturing gold ions and leaving most other metals behind. When this loaded sponge is heated to over 1,000°C (that’s more than 1,800°F!), it shrinks down to metallic flakes. Fused together, these produced a 450-milligram nugget made up of 91% gold and 9% copper—the hallmark of 22-carat gold.
The Economic (and Environmental) Case for Urban Mining
The appetite for this method isn’t just academic. Why so much excitement? The gold content in a motherboard or connector can range between 30 and 100 grams per metric ton—up to a hundred times richer than world-class natural mines. However, the dark side is that many informal recovery channels leave unprotected workers exposed to dangerous chemicals and heavy metals.
To help put this in perspective:
- In 2023, approximately 61.3 million tonnes (metric tons) of e-waste were generated worldwide—a river of discarded gadgets growing faster than any other solid-waste stream.
- Less than 20% of that mountain is officially collected and recycled, according to available 2019 data.
- Each year’s e-waste could line up enough trucks to circle the globe—no exaggeration!
- A single bitcoin transaction creates as much e-waste as manufacturing two iPhones.
Cheese, Tech—and Olympic Medals?
This gold extraction trick using cheese by-products isn’t the only clever use of e-waste. For the Tokyo 2020 Olympics, organizers had the bright idea of recycling the country’s stockpiled electronics to provide all the gold, silver, and bronze needed to make the medals. (Now there’s a podium finish for sustainable thinking.)
Back in the lab, the Swiss team notes that their method doesn’t eliminate the need for harsh chemistry—acids are still essential to dissolve the components. Their big advance is in the next step: how you recapture the gold. Until now, this has been the most expensive and polluting part of the process. But using a cheese-waste derived material makes this step far cheaper and less toxic.
According to Raffaele Mezzenga, the calculated costs of raw materials and energy amount to about one-fiftieth the value of the recovered gold, making the method financially attractive for larger-scale operations. The team is also exploring other food by-products for similar sponge applications, and even looking at ways to extract precious metals from microprocessor manufacturing waste and electrolytic gilding.
The Road Ahead: Scaling Up Sustainable Solutions
It’s still very early days. The protein sponge isn’t about to replace established recycling circuits overnight: so far, there’s no pilot factory on the horizon, just lab results. The real promise lies in tackling what’s currently the most dangerous step in gold recovery—using a waste material already produced in huge quantities by the food industry. Of course, the leap from lab to industrial scale remains the biggest unknown.
So, the next time you’re tempted to toss that old phone, remember: its real value might not just be in nostalgia. As scientists keep discovering, sometimes treasure doesn’t come from gold mines—it comes from the cheese aisle.

### Copper Bulls on the Ropes: Trump's Tariffs Still Missing in Action as Macquarie Warns of a Slide Back to $10,000 a Ton — BigGo Finance

Copper Bulls on the Ropes: Trump's Tariffs Still Missing in Action as Macquarie Warns of a Slide Back to $10,000 a Ton
U.S. President Trump's long-brewing copper import tariffs have yet to produce a clear plan, and the "tariff arbitrage" trade that has run for more than a year is now under strain. With the copper futures spread between New York and London narrowing rapidly, the market is increasingly split over whether the copper bull run can continue. In a new scenario analysis, Macquarie Group warned that if tariffs ultimately fail to materialize, copper could fall back to around $10,000 a ton; conversely, if tariffs are announced with enough of a shipping window, prices could be pushed as high as $18,000 a ton.
This weekend, the annual metals industry gathering LME Week opens in London. The unresolved tariff policy, an imbalanced distribution of global inventories and weakening macro demand are the three key variables as attendees reassess copper's rally.
Spread collapse undermines the arbitrage
Since Trump first formally proposed taxing copper in February 2025, Comex copper has persistently held a significant premium to the LME benchmark, giving traders a strong incentive to ship copper to the United States before tariffs took effect and capture the spread between the two markets. The United States' continued absorption of copper in turn squeezed inventories elsewhere, creating a feedback loop that pushed prices higher and drove LME copper to a record high near $15,000 a ton last month.
On October 12, LME cash copper settled at $14,822 a ton, a record for a cash settlement, and at $14,741 on October 13; LME inventories have fallen for seven consecutive trading days to 229,650 tons.
That logic is now reversing. After reports last month that Trump's copper tariff plan had stalled over inflation concerns, the spread between the two markets has narrowed markedly faster. Data from ship-tracking firm Kpler shows copper shipments from Africa to the United States have begun to decline.
Comex's most-active contract had only just set a settlement record of $6.89 a pound on September 9 when reports the next day that the tariff plan had stalled sent prices down more than 5% — a snapshot of how sensitive the market is to policy signals.
Marc Bailey, chief executive of metals broker Sucden Financial, said in an interview in London that investor interest in the arbitrage trade has picked up again in recent weeks. "Over the past few weeks, sentiment seems to have shifted somewhat. People are starting to think about tentatively re-engaging," he said. Traders are selling Comex contracts and buying LME contracts, betting the two prices will converge.
But this "anti-tariff" bet carries its own risks: Trump could suddenly announce a tariff increase after the midterm elections and before the relevant cargoes reach the United States, inflicting heavy losses on positions betting against implementation.
The spread narrowing in numbers

The more telling shift came in the cash market: in mid-October, the Comex October contract equated to about $6.62 a pound, already below LME cash at $6.69 — meaning Comex had swung to a discount of about $150 a ton. According to John Gross of The Copper Journal, the average spread since October has flipped from a Comex premium of 17 cents a pound in June to an LME premium of 0.85 cents a pound.
Munro noted that tightening near-dated LME copper supply has pushed prices for contracts approaching delivery higher relative to Comex, further compressing the spread. "There's more willingness to sell New York and buy London, because as contracts enter their delivery month, the arbitrage naturally tends to converge," he said by telephone from London. He also noted growing willingness to bet on lower copper prices on macroeconomic grounds.
'One person's decision' and bubble warnings
Copper's heavy dependence on a single policy variable is rare, analysts say. "I've never seen anything like this, where the price outlook ultimately depends on one person's decision," said Alice Fox, a commodities strategist at Macquarie.
Analysts at Natixis SA had earlier warned that the tariff trade has created a bubble in one of the world's most important industrial metals, and that the bubble could burst quickly once tariffs are ruled out.
Traders have been burned on the tariff question before. In July 2025, Trump imposed a 50% tariff on downstream copper products but did not touch the refined copper traded in futures markets, and Comex copper prices fell sharply in response; only after he kept open the possibility of taxing refined copper did New York prices hold a persistent premium.
But the copper market has not formed a consensus bearish view. Some traders still expect Trump to proceed with tariffs, possibly as early as January 1, in line with a recommendation the U.S. Department of Commerce previously submitted to the White House. That recommendation proposed a phased approach: a 15% rate in 2027, rising to 30% in 2028. If pursued, the United States would continue absorbing copper, reigniting a scramble among global buyers for supply.
Macquarie itself, however, considers the most likely scenario not implementation but a quiet pass of the January 1 deadline with no announcement at all — leaving the roughly 1.5 million tons of copper Macquarie estimates the United States holds sitting where it is. Of the firm's four tariff scenarios, "explicit exclusion of tariffs" is rated the least likely; if it did happen, Comex prices would lead a decline, the arbitrage would reverse, and the U.S. surplus would reappear in LME warehouses in the United States and Asia.
Another popular view is that the White House's delay in deciding may be deliberate "strategic ambiguity" aimed at preventing the massive copper stockpile the United States has accumulated from flooding back into international markets.

Inventory glut spawns another trade
Tariff expectations have led the United States to stockpile an astonishing volume of copper that manufacturers may take years to work through. That surplus has itself created a new trading opportunity: traders buy physical copper inventory while selling Comex futures contracts, locking in returns from the contango structure in which futures trade above cash.
Profits from this kind of financing trade are far below the arbitrage gains from earlier copper shipments to the United States, but analysts at Bank of America believe returns are still sufficient to keep traders holding inventory, preventing some copper from flowing back into international markets. In other words, even if Trump ultimately backs down on tariffs, U.S. inventories may not return immediately, and the global supply picture could remain distorted.
"The key uncertainty is U.S. tariff policy. With little visibility into the government's thinking, it's hard to make a direct directional call on whether to be long or short copper," said Grace Li, investment director at Shanghai Ducheng Private Fund Management Co.
Helen Amos, a commodities analyst at BMO Capital Markets, cautioned that the market may be underestimating how long the stockpiling lasts. The bank estimates the United States has stockpiled close to 2 million tons of copper, roughly a full year of U.S. consumption; global visible inventories rose about 300,000 tons this year, but all of the increase was in the United States, while inventories outside the country fell 168,000 tons. Over the same period, China's refined copper imports ran below trend and exports above trend, a net change of about 1.6 million tons, which the bank views as equivalent to Beijing drawing on strategic reserves while Washington builds them. BMO believes current prices of $14,500 to $15,000 a ton are fundamentally supported. "They probably thought this stockpiling would have been over by now. What if they think again: 'Actually, this is going to last another two years'?"
Signals from the physical market
Aurubis, Europe's largest copper producer, has offered customers a premium of $347 a ton for 2027 supply, 10% higher than this year and a record. A company spokesperson said large volumes of copper have been diverted to the United States, leaving European warehouses fairly empty.
Another potential variable is a scrap copper export ban. The market expects any tariff decision could come with restrictions on scrap copper exports; if implemented immediately, about 600,000 tons a year of copper-bearing scrap would leave the global market.
Record copper prices, near-zero treatment charges for Japanese smelters
The direction of copper prices and the earnings of Japanese smelters are not the same thing. Smelter earnings hinge on the copper concentrate treatment and refining charges (TC/RC) paid by miners; higher copper prices benefit miners with their own mines but offer limited help to pure smelters. As Chinese smelting capacity expands and ore supply tightens, the 2026 copper concentrate treatment charge agreed between miner Antofagasta and Chinese smelters has fallen to $0 a ton, with spot treatment charges dropping into negative territory.
That pressure has already driven a restructuring of Japan's copper smelting industry. JX Metals, Mitsubishi Materials, Mitsui Mining & Smelting and Marubeni have agreed to consolidate copper concentrate procurement and electrolytic copper sales into Pan Pacific Copper (PPC), with implementation planned for October 1, 2026, to enlarge purchasing scale and cut costs. Mitsubishi Materials' Onahama smelter has also decided to stop processing copper concentrate, expected by the end of March 2027.
Beyond producing electrolytic copper, Japanese domestic smelters also recover rare and precious metals critical to AI data centers and advanced technologies; JX Metals therefore frames the survival of its smelters as part of economic security. In other words, even with copper prices high, Japanese smelters' profits still depend on whether treatment charges stop falling — a more direct transmission channel for the Japanese market than the broader copper bull-bear debate.
Market impact
For copper bulls, the current rally rests on the expectation that tariffs will eventually take effect. A narrowing spread means weaker economic incentive to ship copper to the United States, and if the policy expectation is disproved, prices previously supported by inventory relocation could face rapid revaluation. At the same time, rising energy prices, higher interest rates and weaker equity markets are threatening the demand outlook for industrial metals, making copper prices even more sensitive to policy signals.
Copper's rally this year is also reflected in mining stocks: Comex copper is up more than 18% year to date, Southern Copper has gained 44% this year and Freeport 37%. RBC Capital Markets estimates that North American copper miners' current share prices imply a copper price of about $5.03 a pound, below futures at about $6.70, meaning the shares have not fully reflected spot prices.
As the world's largest copper consumer, China's restocking pace is another variable. If China is forced to replenish inventories while U.S. imports accelerate again on tariff expectations, the global market could see a repeat of fierce competition for supply, driving prices into a spiral.

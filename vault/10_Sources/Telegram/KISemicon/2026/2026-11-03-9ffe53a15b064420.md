단독솔리다임, 美에 130조 낸드팹 2기 구축
■ SK하닉, 뉴욕주 마시나노센터 낙점
月 10만장 생산 팹 2기 순차 건설
뉴욕주와 투자 조건 막바지 조율
마이크론 팹 인근…인프라 갖춰
SK하이닉스(000660)의 낸드플래시 전문 손자회사 솔리다임이 미국 뉴욕주 오나이다카운티의 산업단지 ‘마시나노센터’에 최대 130조 원을 투자해 낸드 생산 공장을 건설할 것으로 알려졌다. 미국에 첫 낸드 팹을 신설해 인공지능(AI) 데이터센터 붐으로 팽창하는 첨단 낸드 시장의 패권을 쥔다는 전략이다.
3일 업계와 미 뉴욕주에 따르면 SK하이닉스와 솔리다임은 첫 미국 낸드 팹 후보지로 뉴욕주 마시나노센터를 사실상 낙점하고 관계기관들과 투자 조건을 조율하고 있다. 업계 고위 관계자는 “마시나노센터를 유력 후보지로 놓고 협상 중”이라며 “구체적인 투자 규모와 착공 시점, 투자 집행 계획은 협의 결과에 따라 달라질 것”이라고 말했다.
마시나노센터 개발 담당자인 팀 피츠제럴드 모호크밸리 경제개발기관 수석부사장은 서울경제신문에 “오나이다카운티 및 뉴욕주 정부와 마시나노센터에 ‘메이저 기업’을 유치하기 위한 협상에 참여하고 있다”며 솔리다임의 투자 계획을 확인했다.
마시나노센터는 434에이커(175만 6336㎡) 규모로 축구장 246개에 달하는 부지에 반도체 공장을 위한 전력과 용수 인프라를 갖추고 있다. 추가 개발이 가능한 부지도 127에이커에 달한다. 미 마이크론이 D램 생산 거점을 조성하는 뉴욕주 클레이와 차로 약 1시간 거리로, 반도체 공급망 구축과 인력 확보에도 유리하다.
업계에 따르면 솔리다임은 마시나노센터에 12인치 웨이퍼 기준 월 10만 장의 생산능력을 갖춘 낸드 팹 1기를 우선 완공하고 2기까지 늘릴 예정이다. 낸드 팹 1기당 투자비가 400억~500억 달러여서 최대 1000억 달러(약 133조 원)의 투자를 예상하고 있다. 뉴욕주는 솔리다임의 낸드 팹 부지 조성에만 최대 5000만 달러 지원을 검토 중이다.

### Qualcomm Takes On The AI Inference Memory Bottleneck With Working HBC Silicon, And Its First Data Center Product Is Already Taped Out

(https://vo.la/WaYN3P1)

Qualcomm has announced that its first-generation High Bandwidth Compute (HBC) silicon has been validated in its San Diego lab, giving the company early hardware evidence for a memory design aimed at AI inference. The chip seemingly ran the complete memory read-write path and near-memory computations at production-representative dimensions, using industry-standard automated test equipment with randomized weights and activations.
The chipset maker’s Dragonfly AI250 is expected to sample in the first half of 2027
The target is memory bandwidth, which Qualcomm calls a central constraint for LLM inference because every generated token requires model data to be fed to the compute units. When operating at a data center scale, the energy and latency of that movement affect throughput, power consumption, and total cost of ownership. HBC aims to solve this by placing compute closer to memory to cut data movement and raise effective bandwidth.
Qualcomm’s HBC design stacks low-power LPDDR dies on top of an active AI accelerator base die using wafer-to-wafer bonding, which shrinks the distance data travels from millimeters to just micrometers. As reported by HotHardware, this approach results in an 18x increase in effective memory bandwidth over LPDDR5X and more than 5x the bandwidth per watt of projected HBM4 specifications.
However, the efficiency improvements landed slightly below the 6x benchmark Qualcomm had projected earlier. The San Diego firm’s own footnote says the figures are estimates based on measured silicon results normalized to target operating conditions and compared with published competing product specifications, and that final performance may vary by implementation, workload, and system configuration.
Qualcomm also describes a staged program spanning architecture, process development, test vehicles, silicon validation and product integration, and says it has worked with foundry and assembly partners to qualify wafer-to-wafer bonding. To address defects in its stacked layers, the design includes redundancy and remapping, allowing it to work around faulty memory. The chipset maker also points to more than a decade of memory-on-logic work, including commercialized direct bonding of DRAM onto logic and a hand in shaping JEDEC LPDDR standards.
Commercial products from Qualcomm are expected to employ a four-layer DRAM stack, up from a two-layer one. Speaking of products, the Dragonfly AI250 is targeted for sampling in the first half of 2027 and will slot into Qualcomm’s Dragonfly Rack inference infrastructure. As for the specifications, the hardware includes a fully liquid-cooled rack with 38.6 TB of memory and 7.4 petabytes per second of effective bandwidth.
Qualcomm’s in-house launch strategy will move towards the server side as it plans to deploy its Oryon-based C1000 server CPU in 2027, followed by next-generation HBC in the Dragonfly AI300 in 2028. While it’s certainly an improvement, it’s important to keep in mind that these results are from Qualcomm’s own lab based on early silicon, and there’s a possibility that independent benchmarks paint a different picture, which we’ll update readers about in the future.

### Don't Expect Cheaper SSDs Anytime Soon: HBF Would Cost Just $0.64 Per GB But Consume 6.5x More Wafers Than A Typical TLC NAND

(https://vo.la/0iKcTw5)

If High Bandwidth Flash or HBF proves to be a commercial success, it would likely preclude cheaper SSDs for the foreseeable future, especially as HBF presumably entails just one-tenth of the cost of a comparable HBM4 stack, leading to a Jevons paradox-driven incremental demand.
An 8-Hi stack of HBF will cost $0.64 per GB vs. the $6.78 per GB cost for an 8-Hi HBM4 stack
For the benefit of those who might not be aware, just as HBM stacks DRAM, HBF stacks NAND dies on top of each other, with Through Silicon Vias (TSVs) connecting the dies together and a controller logic die bonded to this NAND array.
The problem with using NAND cells, however, is their turtle-like speeds. An SRAM offers read speeds of just around a nanosecond vs. ~100 nanoseconds for DRAM and a whopping ~100 microseconds for NAND. This means that NAND offers 1,000x slower read speeds than a DRAM-based HBM.
HBF, however, leverages the power of parallelism to increase bandwidth by orders of magnitude. Basically, the logic die schedules thousands of parallel reads of NAND cells simultaneously. So, while each individual read of a given NAND cell is around 1,000x slower, thousands of parallel reads can deliver a cumulative bandwidth of 0.3TB/s to 3TB/s.
Even so, an HBF can't counter NAND's atrocious write speeds and the attendant fragile write endurance. At any rate, SanDisk is currently developing an HBF standard with SK hynix, one that would offer 512GB of storage along with a bandwidth of 0.4TB/s to 3TB/s, with a commercial rollout expected by 2028/2029.
This brings us to the core of today's topic. The analysts at Bernstein have now estimated that a typical 8-Hi HBF stack will cost just $0.64 per GB, which is around one-tenth the cost of a comparable HBM4 stack that entails a price of around $6.78 per GB.
Given this substantial cost differential, HBF can feasibly displace HBM in several use cases, such as storing model weights or when dealing with shared prefix KV caching - where thousands of distinct user prompts often query the exact same massive system prompt, codebase, or multi-hundred-page documentation context; the KV cache for this foundational "prefix" data is computed once and then written permanently to HBF.
However, each 8-Hi HBF stack uses 6.5x more wafers than a typical TLC NAND, which would likely squeeze the available supply of NAND wafers once HBF enters the commercialization phase, precluding cheaper SSDs.

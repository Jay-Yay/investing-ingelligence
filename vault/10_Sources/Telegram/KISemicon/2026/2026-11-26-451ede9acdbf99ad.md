### Ohio OKs gas plant for data centers despite local voter backlash • Ohio Capital Journal

(https://vo.la/iQDSSG7)

Ohio OKs gas plant for data centers despite local voter backlash
The approval is drawing fresh criticism of Ohio’s permitting process, which has seen renewable energy projects denied or blocked due to local objections.
Ashville, Ohio (Aesopposea, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)
This story was originally published by Canary Media.
Ohio regulators just greenlit construction of a big gas plant to power a data center complex, despite local residents’ overwhelming rejection of the projects in this month’s elections.
The permit approval itself is not unique: Around the U.S., energy-hungry data center developers are racing ahead with controversial plans to build natural gas–fired generation. But critics of the recent Ohio ruling say it highlights the state’s leniency toward fossil-fuel projects compared with its tight restrictions on solar and wind farms, which are often denied permits due to local opposition.
“There’s a huge double standard for fossil fuels and renewables in the state of Ohio,” said state Rep. Tristan Rader, a Democrat who serves as ranking minority member on the Ohio House Energy Committee and took part in the case as a nonvoting member of the Ohio Power Siting Board.
The gas plant in question is an 800-megawatt facility to be built at a data center complex under development in the central Ohio village of Ashville. As of now, the plant won’t be connected to the grid at large and will serve just the computing warehouses. The project site is close to homes, as well as a playground, park, and soccer field, Rader noted.
“This plant is literally going to be throwing a shadow on these communities,” while spewing health-harming pollution, Rader said during the Power Siting Board’s Nov. 19 meeting. Yet Ohio has more stringent requirements for wind and solar, which emit zero air pollution.
Ashville’s Village Council passed a resolution in April to back the data center and gas plant project, after its developer, EdgeConneX, agreed to pay up to $102 million to support municipal, school, and community work. But the deal didn’t sit well with some residents, who gathered enough signatures for a referendum on whether to overturn the resolution.
On Election Day, Ashville was one of dozens of U.S. communities to reject data centers, with 81% of the village’s voters opposing the local government’s agreement with EdgeConneX.
Even so, the Power Siting Board’s decision OK’ing the gas plant finds that the developer met a statutory requirement that the project is in the public interest. Regulators pointed to factors like the project’s economic benefits, backing from the Village Council and local school district, and the developer’s promises to limit noise and construction hours.
That’s sparked frustration from clean energy advocates in the state. The board has denied multiple solar projects on the basis that local opposition outweighs economic benefits or has proposed permit conditions aimed at addressing community concerns, said Chris Tavenor, general counsel for the Ohio Environmental Council, which was not a party to the case.
“In this case, the Board does the exact opposite, using those community benefits and conditions to justify ignoring local concerns,” he said in an email.
In this decision and those pertaining to renewable energy, critics take issue with the Power Siting Board’s deference to local governments, even when evidence shows they are not necessarily reflecting the wishes of the broader public.
In 2025, for example, the Power Siting Board’s staff recommended against the Grange Solar project in Logan County on the basis of pushback from local governments. But a majority of individuals who submitted comments wanted the solar farm. The developer ultimately withdrew its plan and opted to pursue projects elsewhere.
And earlier this year, regulators nixed the Crossroads Solar farm in Morrow County because all nearby local governments opposed the project. Although that ruling noted the “presence of strong, united opposition” among the local population, dozens of comments were apparently fake, and an analysis by the developer found that the number of verifiable individuals against the project was not overwhelming in comparison with its supporters.
The board’s staff also did an about-face from supporting Crossroads Solar to opposing it after one township changed its position from neutral to opposed.
“Ultimately, the Ohio Power Siting Board has demonstrated its hypocrisy,” Tavenor said, “by approving a natural gas facility for a data center where it has denied renewable energy projects with less significant opposition.”
Regulators, for their part, acknowledged the Ashville vote in their decision but asserted that they have authority to issue the permit regardless of the outcome of the election. The 108-page opinion also says that conditions agreed to by the village, the developer, and the board staff were enough to deal with public concerns.
The board also gave short shrift to opponents’ argument that the gas plant proposal fails to meet a separate legal requirement that projects have minimal environmental impact. Its take stems from the fact that the developer opted for reciprocating internal combustion engines and simple-cycle natural gas turbines over less-polluting combined-cycle turbines or fuel cells, which are more expensive right now.
Regulatory staff argued that “minimum” doesn’t demand the lowest environmental impact but depends on a plant’s purpose, available technology, and economics. The board decided that the project meets the requirement, basically punting to the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency’s expected issuance of air permits for the facility.
Project opponents have 30 days to ask for reconsideration of the ruling. Unless the Power Siting Board changes its mind, an appeal to the Ohio Supreme Court could follow.

Executive Summary 
For industry analysts, there is nothing more satisfying than raising long-
term forecasts that had once seemed aggressive, if not unattainable. That 
is where we find ourselves today with U.S. Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF) 
Assets Under Management (AUM). Growth over the past couple of years 
has exceeded our prior bull case driven by accelerating flows, with an 
added, but secondary, kicker of impressive market returns.  
In this Must C report, we revamp our U.S. ETF AUM forecasting 
methodology and introduce new targets. Our work calls for $25 trillion in 
assets by the end of this decade, with total industry AUM surpassing the 
$40 trillion mark in ten years as Active ETF market share doubles over that 
span. The breakdown of growth expectations between organic (i.e. flows) 
and inorganic (i.e. performance) drivers is consistent with a view that U.S. 
ETFs are in a more mature growth phase.
 
Specifically, we expect 12% annualized growth over the next decade that is 
balanced between flows and return influences. This compares to the last ten 
years where flows drove a more significant portion of the 20% compounding 
AUM growth.   
Underlying the impressive industry growth story is the surge in Active ETFs. 
We expect this tailwind to persist and make Active a key focus in this deep 
dive. Our base case expects Active’s market share of ETF AUM to double in 
ten years as these products gain greater share of industry flows. This will not 
fully offset losses to Index/Passive strategies in Mutual Funds but will be a 
new growth driver for Active which has been under pressure more broadly. 
Importantly, the dual theme of growing Active ETF ownership plus increased 
trading use of Index/Passive products for portfolio adjustments highlight 
the longer-term growth opportunity for U.S.-listed ETFs overall. 
Inside, we highlight three industry trends that benefit Active share gains. 
First, end users have been adopting more sophisticated products at a faster 
pace. Second, core assets are seeing differentiation to better align with 
broader portfolio goals as indices drift. Third, investors are looking for 
differentiated means of generating satellite characteristics around core 
exposures, such as cyclicality, secular growth, and income. These play well 
to Active for security identification, risk management, or liquidity sourcing. 
Last, we incorporate the input of our Citi colleagues across Wealth, Investor 
Services, and Markets divisions to exemplify how the ecosystem involving 
Active ETFs is supporting growth. The overarching takeaway is Active ETFs 
require a level of complexity that the initial Index/Passive products did not. 
Issuers need to prove they are capable of not just managing underlying 
strategies but also appreciate how to fully use and manage features of the 
ETF wrapper (the investment structure that holds the underlying assets). 
Here, information flow with different participants in the ecosystem helps 
create a self-sustaining dynamic.  
Ultimately, the future of the U.S. ETF industry is increasingly down a 
solutions path. Next generation ETFs will need to deliver end users the tools 
to mass customize financial services or create internal efficiencies within 
more complex portfolios. This implies the next leg of more mature growth 
will be a function of professional ETF management. No doubt, unfolding 
tactical themes will emerge, however a longer-term focus on differentiated 
solutions will be increasingly critical over the next decade.

Rightsizing the Industry Outlook 
U.S. ETF industry AUM growth has exceeded our previous bull case 
assumptions. This pushes us to revisit and raise our estimates. Our new 
base case calls for $25 trillion in assets by the end of this decade, with the 
industry breaching the $40 trillion mark in ten years. While these 
projections are more optimistic than our prior estimates, it still suggests 
ETFs will be in a more mature phase of AUM growth as flows (organic) and 
performance (inorganic) drivers will be more balanced than the previous 
ten years.  
A year ago, we opined on the roadmap to the next $10 trillion in U.S. ETF 
industry assets. The core thesis was that ETFs would continue to rapidly 
take share from Mutual Funds as new product innovation driven by 
professional industry leaders would lead to greater availability and scaling 
of more finely tuned investment management.  A key component of this 
was a slowly unfolding battle for the core of asset allocation approaches 
whereby Active management could gain share from traditional Index 
approaches. Part of that argument was a perspective that highly 
concentrated and widely followed Equity benchmarks would bear more 
idiosyncrasies, leading investors to more differentiated and active 
approaches to generate targeted portfolio characteristics. As for Fixed 
Income and other asset classes, recognition that index approaches often 
overlooked or underweighted a variety of unique sub-categories that 
generated differentiated return streams has increased. The latter has 
gained even more traction via Active ETFs as liquidity ecosystems rapidly 
evolve, improve, democratize, and even create new investable universes, 
some of which we commented on last year.  
In our first Must C publications on ETFs, we highlighted further 
encroachment of ETFs on Mutual Funds, especially outside of defined 
contribution gates (see 
Sizing the ETF Opportunity: From Passive 
Revolution to Active Evolution). T o us, this was not just going to be a 
continued wave of index adoption, but a new push with Active 
differentiation in the ETF industry. Innovation within asset classes, 
delivering new strategies, and the opening of new investable corners of the 
global opportunity set would unfold via ETFs with more of an active tilt.  
In combination, those two cornerstone views on the U.S. ETF industry 
were undoubtedly bullish. However, its continued growth has exceeded 
even our optimistic estimates in these past few years driven largely by 
organic growth (i.e. flows), not just impressive risk asset returns. This leads 
us to reassess our approach to forecasting aggregate U.S. ETF AUM up 
front in this report.

In summary, our prior ETF AUM assumptions proved to be conservative as 
data in recent years exceeded even our bull case. This was largely a 
function of stronger flows, not just impressive risk asset returns. Therefore, 
we adjust our framework, focusing on forecasting flows as a percentage of 
AUM. This leads us to seemingly more aggressive estimates for ETF AUM in

$25 trillion in U.S. ETF AUM looks attainable by the end of this decade,

decade of industry growth versus the last decade is likely more balance 
between organic (flows) and inorganic (performance) growth drivers. 
The Active Trajectory

will more than double to 21% from 10% currently. Active ETFs have been 
gaining a larger share of flows in recent years. T o some degree, this should

definition removes ETFs that are designed to generate a prescribed profile

meaning Active share of U.S. ETFs is approximately 10%.

Specific Active Tailwinds 
Active ETFs are already seeing some early tailwinds that are supportive of 
further share gains. First, investors have been increasingly adopting more 
sophisticated products that either have more difficult to access exposures, 
evaluate or trade underlying securities, or employ differentiated overlays 
and expression instruments. Second, ETF users are increasingly looking for 
alternative core exposures, likely a function of index drift over time in both 
Fixed Income, and, more recently, Equity. Third, market participants are 
looking for differentiated return profiles to generate some of the most 
popular satellite characteristics added around core allocations. 
In the sections above we upsized our overall U.S. ETF industry AUM targets 
while projecting that Active will double in its share of aggregate assets in 
the next decade. Herein, we look at some of the key subplots in that 
broader industry narrative. 
The importance of scalable portfolio construction is key. Said differently, 
models or home office solutions will be key for the largest ETF users. 
Competition in this layer of the financial services value chain requires a 
degree of customization as portfolio solution providers, or even the 
institutions trying to meet their own needs, demand a greater variety of 
tools via ETFs to support their clients and business goals.  
Three key underlying trends that benefit stand out to us: 1) the demand for 
more sophisticated strategies; 2) more differentiation within core holdings; 
and 3) increased willingness to use active to generate popular Satellite 
characteristics. We dig into these subplots below, and use our own, 
differentiated classification schemes for the underlying products in the 
U.S. ETF universe.  
Increasing Sophistication 
We start with product sophistication. This is not well defined in the 
industry, but the trend is clearly recognizable with more differentiated 
products becoming available via ETFs. We categorize every U.S.-listed ETF 
as having either High, Medium, or Low sophistication based on the 
complexity of the strategy employed, as well as the difficulty trading or 
managing the underlying asset class. Basically, long-only products with 
minimum tracking error to broad benchmarks in more liquid asset classes 
will score as Low, while derivatives-based ETFs or those with more of a 
liquidity mismatch with Equity exchanges will score as High or Medium.

How does servicing differ for an Index ETF versus Active products? 
For traditional Index ETFs, the underlying holdings are generally well-
known and change infrequently. This predictability allows an index fund to 
have a basket of securities that is relatively static. Index funds also 
commonly use the same basket for creation, redemption, and pricing. In 
contrast, Active ETFs require much more dynamic servicing. It is far more 
common to see multiple baskets with different compositions for Active 
ETFs such as one for creation, one for redemption, one with everything for 
pricing, and there may be custom baskets on any given day.  
How do service requirements differ for Active Equity, Fixed Income, 
traditional (i.e. long only), and complex (i.e. derivatives) strategies? 
When considering service requirements for Active ETFs, distinct 
differences emerge between asset classes and strategy complexities. 
For Fixed Income ETFs, service needs are primarily driven by the prevalence 
of negotiated orders. Unlike Equity baskets, each Fixed Income order often 
results in a unique basket composition. The increasing volume of these 
negotiated transactions, coupled with a notable shift away from cash 
orders to more in-kind transactions, necessitates robust and flexible 
operational capabilities to handle diverse and complex settlement 
processes. This demands a service model capable of managing higher 
variability and volume in transaction types. 
Conversely, more complex and derivative-based ETFs traditionally see a 
higher proportion of cash orders. However, there's an evolving trend where 
in-kind transactions are becoming more common, even for asset classes 
like options. This indicates that while they might have historically had 
simpler processing for basket creation, their service requirements are now 
converging with the complexities seen in Fixed Income, demanding 
adaptable infrastructure for managing a wider range of asset types in-
kind. 
Both Fixed Income and complex/derivative ETFs present unique, and 
increasingly intricate, service requirements that move beyond traditional 
long-only Equity models, emphasizing the need for flexible, efficient, and 
sophisticated operational support for varied transaction types and asset 
classes. 
How is information flow changing for Active ETF managers? 
For Active ETF managers, the landscape of information flow is undergoing 
a significant transformation, demanding faster and more real-time data 
access. Historically, Index ETFs and mutual funds often updated their 
Investment Book of Record (IBOR) at the end of the trading day. However, 
many Active ETF managers are now updating for in-kind transactions 
intraday, as orders are received throughout the session. This shift is driven 
by the critical need for immediate and accurate portfolio visibility.  
Given the dynamic nature of active management, delays in data 
processing can impact investment decisions and operational efficiency. 
Consequently, speed in making these updates has become paramount. In 
essence, the information flow for Active ETF managers is rapidly evolving 
towards a highly automated, API-driven ecosystem, enabling real-time 
data ingestion and processing.

How has the Markets organization adapted to the rise of Active ETFs? 
The proliferation of Active ETFs coming to market has placed extraordinary 
demands on trading desks to coordinate across product lines — requiring a 
fundamentally more integrated operating model within Markets. 
As the universe of Active ETF strategies expands rapidly, the imperative for 
cross-desk alignment has never been greater. Engagement across the 
trading floor — and the cultivation of genuine synergies between 
businesses servicing issuers — is no longer a competitive advantage. It is 
the baseline requirement for a successful product lineup, for both issuer 
and broker alike. 
The surge in multi-asset structures is a defining feature of this evolution. 
Products that incorporate derivatives, equity swaps, and traditional 
equities within a single wrapper demand cohesive servicing across 
multiple touchpoints simultaneously. Siloed execution is not an option; 
the complexity of these instruments requires that every desk speaks the 
same language and operates toward a unified outcome. 
The ETF revolution is also not a sell-side story alone. Buy-side trading 
desks — long defined by their separation from capital markets functions — 
are now actively integrating ETF capital markets expertise and trading 
capabilities under a unified operating model. 
How have broker services changed during this ETF evolution? 
At its foundation, the broker's value proposition has not fundamentally 
changed. Execution and rebalancing remain at the core of broker services. 
Whether the vehicle is an Active ETF or a Mutual Fund, the underlying 
mechanics of trade execution and portfolio management are largely 
interchangeable. The wrapper changes; the expertise does not.  
The proliferation of Active ETF launches has introduced a structurally 
distinct demand: primary market servicing. Unlike Mutual Funds, ETFs 
require a functioning ecosystem of Authorized Participants (APs) to 
operate efficiently — and the street's appetite for capable APs has grown 
sharply alongside the surge in new product launches.

Beyond this, a notable shift is underway in how ETF products are

How are creation/redemption processes different between Index and 
Active ETFs? 
The creation/redemption mechanism is largely the same for all ETFs 
however, the nature of the changes to the underlying basket of securities 
differs significantly between Index/Passive and Active ETFs.   
Index/Passive ETFs have dispersed and predictable overnight basket 
changes that are driven by the rebalancing of the index they track.   
Active ETFs have more distinct and concentrated basket changes that are 
the result of the portfolio manager's discretionary investment decisions, 
which can involve significant adjustments to individual holdings. 
This distinction is a key factor for investors to consider when choosing 
between Index/Passive and Active ETFs, as it can impact the fund's trading 
costs, tracking error (for Index/Passive funds), and overall performance.

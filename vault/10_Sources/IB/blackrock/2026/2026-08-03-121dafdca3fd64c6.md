Brent crude oil surged back to around $117 a barrel last week, its highest since 
April. But oil prices tell only part of the story of how the energy shock has evolved. 
The initial disruption centered on crude supply, but rerouted shipments, 
inventories and strategic reserves helped absorb much of the shock. Now scarcity 
has shifted downstream. Even as crude supplies recover, refining and shipping 
bottlenecks have pushed the delivered cost of fuel from the Middle East to Asia to 
more than twice prewar levels. This helps explain why shares of refiners have 
jumped some 160% this year, while shares of tanker operators have climbed 
120%. See the chart. Both have far outpaced the 40% gain in major oil and gas 
producers, even as energy has become the top-performing sector globally. But 
after such sharp gains, how much further these stocks can run depends on which 
bottlenecks persist, even if the conflict ends.

The Mideast energy shock’s next phase
• The Middle East conflict is exposing longer-term energy constraints as buffers 
that absorbed the initial shock weaken. Selectivity is key in this environment. 
• The Nasdaq fell nearly 1% last week and U.S. Treasuries rallied as investors 
sought safety from the tech stock sell-off. Brent crude oil hit a five-month high. 
• We eye the Fed’s second-to last policy meeting of 2026. We expect another hike 
by year-end but see market pricing for further tightening as overly hawkish.                                                                                                                             
The global economy and markets have proved resilient to the Middle East energy 
shock. Strong AI investment and fiscal spending have cushioned the hit to growth, 
while supply buffers helped absorb much of the initial shock. Now, renewed attacks 
targeting energy sites and shipping in the Gulf are testing these buffers just as 
they’re thinning, while supply constraints are moving downstream. Selectivity is 
key as new vulnerabilities emerge across regions, sectors and asset classes.

Global growth has held up thanks to strong AI investment and government spending. Much of the initial inflation shock is 
already reflected in headline inflation, but higher fuel costs are starting to feed into some core inflation categories. As we’ve 
previously noted, the energy shock has uneven effects: We estimate it’s reduced GDP by about 0.5% in Europe, compared 
with just 0.1% in the U.S. Those differences could become even more pronounced. Europe looks particularly vulnerable, with 
natural gas storage at around 75% – its lowest seasonal level since recordkeeping began in 2011 – while disrupted supplies 
of liquefied natural gas complicate efforts to rebuild reserves. A colder winter could push European energy prices, already up 
170% this year, higher. The U.S. is better insulated as a net energy exporter, while strong tech exports have cushioned Asia 
from higher import costs. The shock’s uneven impacts are accelerating efforts to bolster energy resilience and security. 
That is creating new investment opportunities – but at a cost. The geopolitical fragmentation mega force we’ve long 
described reinforces this shift, raising the value of infrastructure that protects against future shocks. Energy importers are 
diversifying both traditional fuel supplies and electrification, underscoring the energy transition and resilience mega force. 
Exporters, including those in the Middle East, are investing in alternative routes to bypass vulnerable chokepoints. We see 
this as a structural shift that could outlast the conflict. Yet building these contingencies requires substantial investment, 
which could intensify competition for capital and put further upward pressure on sovereign bond yields. 
We favor opportunities where energy constraints and profit margins look durable and valuations remain attractive. For now, 
existing energy equity exposures could deliver strong earnings as oil prices and refining margins stay elevated. But after this 
year’s oil rally, we also see opportunities in select U.S. and European utilities hit by rising bond yields. In bonds, higher yields 
support our recent upgrade to U.S. Treasuries. We prefer short-term bonds for income, as growing energy security investment 
could increase competition for capital and push longer-term yields higher.
Bottom line: The Mideast energy shock is exposing longer-term constraints that could outlast the conflict. We favor selective 
exposure to energy infrastructure and short-term income over broad market bets.

Market backdrop 
Tech stocks swung sharply last week as investors stepped up scrutiny of the AI trade, pushing the Nasdaq down roughly 1% 
despite strong earnings from some of the sector’s biggest names. The S&P 500 fell 0.4%, with a rotation into defensive 
sectors helping limit losses. U.S. 10-year Treasury yields slipped below 5% as investors sought refuge from the tech sell-off. 
Brent crude oil touched a six-month high of $117 a barrel on concerns about further disruption to Middle East energy flows.

and opens up alpha opportunities. They cut across asset class labels, spurring a rethink of portfolio construction. Investors need to 
be deliberate about the economic or thematic exposures they own, the vehicles they use to implement them and their investment 
horizons.

Equities
Developed markets
United States We are neutral. Strong AI fundamentals remain supportive, but policy uncertainty could fuel 
near-term volatility. We continue to favor AI and tech.
Europe We are neutral. We would need to see more business-friendly policy and deeper capital markets 
for Europe to outperform. We favor financials, infrastructure and industrials.
UK We are neutral. Valuations remain attractive relative to the U.S., but we see few near-term 
catalysts to trigger a shift.
Japan We are neutral. Strong corporate balance sheets and governance reforms remain supportive. 
We prefer targeted exposures to physical AI and the buildout’s bottlenecks. 
Emerging markets We are overweight. Strong earnings and cheaper valuations create opportunities. We 
particularly like different expressions of the AI scarcity theme across Asia and Latin America.
China We are neutral. We see opportunities in physical AI. Cheap, open-source AI could drive adoption, 
but that doesn’t necessarily translate into AI-provider profitability.
Short U.S. Treasuries
We are overweight. Much of the repricing we anticipated has occurred, and expectations for 
further Fed hikes look excessive. We favor the front end for attractive income and a greater 
breakeven buffer.
Long U.S. Treasuries We are neutral. Higher yields offer attractive income and a bigger cushion against further rate 
increases. We still see room for yields to rise as competition for capital intensifies.
Fixed Income
Global inflation-linked 
bonds
We are neutral. We see inflation settling above pre-pandemic levels, but markets may not price 
this in the near term as economic growth could slow.
Euro area govt bonds
We are neutral on short- and medium-term bonds. Current ECB rate pricing looks fairly valued 
given the balance of risks. Higher energy prices could push rates higher. We prefer to deploy 
risk elsewhere. 
UK gilts We are neutral. We expect periods of elevated volatility given political uncertainty, longer-term 
bonds making up a larger market share, and buyers becoming more price-sensitive.
Japanese govt bonds We are underweight. The substantial repricing in yields has improved the risk-reward, but 
further rate hikes and heavy issuance could push yields higher.
China govt bonds We are neutral. China bonds offer stability and diversification but developed market yields are 
higher. A shift in investor sentiment toward equities limits upside. 
U.S. agency MBS We are neutral and lean into the stable income MBS provide despite tight spreads.
Short-term IG credit We are neutral. Spreads are tight due to corporate strength, but attractive overall yields help 
offset the risk of increased issuance.
Long-term IG credit We are underweight. We prefer short-term bonds less exposed to interest rate risk over long-
term bonds. But attractive overall yields help offset the risk of increased issuance.
Global high yield We are neutral. High yield offers attractive income. We prefer higher-rated U.S. and European 
high yield over investment grade and see dispersion of returns increasing. 
Asia credit We are neutral. Overall yields are attractive and fundamentals are solid, but spreads are tight. 
Emerging hard currency We are neutral. Fundamentals have improved, but we see a more attractive risk-reward profile in 
EM local debt.
Emerging local currency We are overweight. We like the yield relative to its volatility and improving fundamentals.

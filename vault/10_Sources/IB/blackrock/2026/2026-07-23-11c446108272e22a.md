Foreword
More yield, more judgment
Higher yields have changed the fixed income 
conversation. The challenge is no longer simply 
finding income. It is understanding what risks 
investors are being paid to take.
That is an enviable problem to have. Today’s 
yields allow more of a portfolio’s potential 
return to come from income rather than relying 
on falling rates or further spread compression. 
But abundance does not make the choices 
easier. Stronger nominal growth, persistent 
inflation risks, geopolitical uncertainty and 
greater competition for capital are pulling 
markets in different directions. Even some 
of the diversification properties investors 
have traditionally expected from bonds are 
being tested.
The opportunity set is richer, but the choices 
within it matter more. Economic and policy 
cycles are diverging, valuations vary widely, and 
investments offering similar yields can carry 
very different underlying risks. That puts a 
greater premium on looking beneath the yield.
Income may be abundant, but it is not 
interchangeable. Assessing the risks behind that 
income may matter as much as the yield itself.
That is the perspective we bring to this quarter’s 
Fixed Income Outlook: looking across global 
markets for resilient sources of income while 
remaining deliberate about where and how risk 
is taken.

Higher rates, increasingly divergent effects
After initially waiting for greater clarity on 
inflation, the Federal Reserve (Fed) raised its 
policy rate by 25 basis points in September, to a 
range of 3.75% to 4.00%, amid stronger inflation 
and labor market data. The move was widely 
anticipated, with the Federal Open Market 
Committee citing inadequate progress on 
underlying inflation and seeking greater 
confidence that inflation will return to target. 
Chair Warsh also pointed to strong growth data in 
arguing that policy is not restrictive. 
We agree that the economy has been remarkably 
resilient overall and that modest further rate 
increases may not put aggregate growth at risk. 
But some parts of the economy are far more 
rate-sensitive than others.
Not all inflation responds to rates
Lower- and middle-income households are more 
reliant on credit than wealthier households, and 
with 90-day credit card delinquencies already at 
12.9%, we worry that higher rates could add to 
their financial stress.1 The Fed remains committed 
to returning inflation to its 2% target and wants to 
see greater progress. Core personal consumption 
expenditures (PCE) inflation has shown little 
recent progress toward that level, leaving the 
overnight policy rate as the Fed’s primary tool 
to address it (see chart on the right).
The more important question for policy is not 
simply how high inflation is, but how responsive 
it is to rates. An analysis from the San Francisco 
Federal Reserve shows cyclical components are 
generally well behaved, while acyclical inflation 
remains harder to contain and less responsive 
to interest rates. Energy, insurance, health care 
and education are passing through higher costs, 
leaving the Fed to combat those pressures with 
a tool poorly suited to the task.

Higher rates, increasingly divergent effects
5
Strong growth, narrow foundations
While growth appears strong overall, its drivers have become increasingly 
narrow and sector-specific. An artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure 
buildout is driving a capital expenditure boom, alongside strong services 
consumption from higher-income households. These areas are relatively 
insensitive to rates, widening the divide with rate-sensitive parts of the 
economy, many of which are already in recession. We believe that divergence 
will increasingly complicate both monetary policy and investment decisions.
With both policy rates and longer-term yields moving higher, the critical 
question for investors is how much risk higher rates pose to rate-sensitive 
parts of the economy and the assets tied to them. Housing is a clear example: 
higher borrowing costs can weigh on activity and create stress in mortgage 
and real estate assets. That makes selectivity increasingly critical, along with 
deep, sector-specific research into which parts of the economy may benefit 
from these trends and which may be impaired by them.
Starting yield changes the equation
For all the debate about why interest rates are at current levels, the more 
useful question for investors is: what return can be generated from this 
starting point? The answer is compelling. What we believe makes today’s 
opportunity unusually attractive is that investors do not need to reach far for 
that income. It is now possible to build a diversified fixed income portfolio 
yielding more than 7%, with less than three years of duration and an average 
credit rating of A-. That said, at these higher interest rate levels, rollover risk, 
or refinancing risk, is a meaningful worry for some segments of the fixed 
income market, which again suggests that selectivity and deep sector 
research is critical to managing where to most effectively attain yield for 
portfolios.
The combination of income, quality and limited interest-rate sensitivity is 
historically unusual. If inflation continues to move closer to the Fed’s 2% 
target, that income becomes even more meaningful in real terms. While 
equities still matter for long-term growth, investors no longer need them to 
carry as much of the return burden. Built deliberately, that combination can 
create a portfolio that does not depend on the next payroll print, consumer 
price index (CPI) surprise or geopolitical headline.

The Federal Open Market Committee’s (FOMC) 
September rate hike confirmed a shift that had 
been building since July. Rising geopolitical 
risk and disruptions to crude shipping pushed 
international oil prices to their highest levels

diesel, have risen well above earlier peaks, while

showing up in its relationship with interest rates. 
Historically, the correlation between oil and the

to roughly 80%.2

yields. Competition for capital and stronger 
global growth are two others. We would add 
a fourth: reduced demand for long-duration 
bonds as their diversification benefits diminish.

When bonds hedge less
For roughly two decades before COVID, persistently negative stock-bond 
correlation coincided with a similarly persistent period of too little inflation. 
Repeated demand shocks, secular stagnation and a low natural rate of 
interest frequently pushed policy rates toward the effective lower bound, 
constraining the Fed’s ability to respond to negative demand shocks.3 
With inflation not constraining the Fed, markets came to expect rates to fall 
in response to negative demand shocks. Monetary accommodation could 
then support growth and inflation in the same direction, the so-called 
“divine coincidence.” Falling rates therefore tended to cushion equity 
declines, contributing to negative stock-bond correlation.
Today’s environment is different. With too much rather than too little 
inflation, the Fed faces tradeoffs between its objectives. Persistent 
inflation shocks can push bond yields higher even as stocks fall, producing 
more frequent bouts of positive stock-bond correlation and weakening 
bonds’ role as an equity hedge. More than five years of above-target inflation 
have been shaped by a sequence of supply-side shocks — from COVID and 
Ukraine to tariffs and, this year, the Iran conflict and AI investment boom. 
The resulting loss of diversification is itself a reason yields need to be higher: 
reduced demand for bonds requires greater income compensation to clear 
the market.
Competition for capital is rising
Nominal GDP provides a useful anchor for long-term interest rates, with 
nominal yields historically tracking nominal growth over time (see chart 
below). Whether yields trade above or below that anchor, however, also 
depends on the supply of and demand for investment capital. Before COVID, 
excess savings relative to investment demand produced a persistent discount 
of Treasury yields to nominal growth. Today, that balance is shifting.

Higher yields, for good reason
8
AI is an important part of that shift. Rising expectations for agentic AI use 
cases are driving estimates of required capex higher, while more of that 
financing is migrating from banks and private markets into public bond 
markets. Treasury issuance therefore competes against growing private-
sector financing needs for investor capital.
Recent rate moves also reflect this competition for capital, not simply higher 
inflation expectations. While the Fed remains focused on the inflationary 
effects of oil and distillates, breakeven inflation has largely looked through 
those pressures. The rise in longer-term yields has instead been driven 
more by higher real rates — consistent with investors demanding greater 
real compensation as investment demand rises.
What higher yields mean from here
The recent global move in rates broadly corresponds with stronger nominal 
growth expectations. Since the start of the year, 30-year government bond 
yields across our sample have risen by roughly 50 basis points on average, 
alongside a similar increase in 2026 nominal GDP growth forecasts.4
What does that mean for rates going forward? Upgrades to nominal 
GDP forecasts reflect the strength, resilience and breadth of the AI capital 
expenditure wave alongside wealth-effect support for consumption. 
Higher oil and distillates prices contribute to a stickier global inflation 
outlook. Rates are adjusting upward to this configuration of strong growth 
and sticky inflation.
Absent further acceleration in either factor, that adjustment should eventually 
slow. But continued uncertainty around oil and signs of accelerating benefits 
from the AI boom could sustain upward pressure on global rates. Higher 
yields reinforce fixed income’s traditional role as a source of income, even as 
persistent excess inflation challenges its role as a diversifier. That reinforces 
the need for new sources of diversification and a rethink of bond allocations 
built primarily around the diversification use case.

year, with German Bund yields up nearly 100 basis 
points since the escalation of the Middle East

Within European credit, investors have increasingly favored defensive 
sectors amid concerns about higher energy prices and slower growth. But 
resilient consumers, fiscal support and improving corporate fundamentals 
could make periods of weakness in consumer-facing sectors an opportunity 
to add selectively. Dispersion is also evident within individual issuers: 
corporate hybrids in utilities, telecoms and energy have lagged the broader 
compression in spreads, creating relative value opportunities across capital 
structures. Meanwhile, renewed rating dispersion is creating greater scope 
for selection across the quality spectrum.
High yield offers another source of differentiation. Fiscal spending 
programs are beginning to support infrastructure, defense and transport, 
while valuations in cyclical sectors such as chemicals and industrials 
already reflect a challenging backdrop. If activity stabilizes, selected 
issuers in these sectors could benefit. We also see value in selected B-rated 
issuers, including in real estate, financials and services, where we believe 
fundamentals remain stronger than current pricing suggests.
Curve selection matters as well. Strong demand for fixed-maturity 
products has supported the front end, while the four- to seven-year 
segment continues to offer attractive carry and relative value. The strength 
of that demand is already evident in Italy, where bond funds have gathered 
approximately €100 billion since 2023, with 75% of inflows directed 
toward fixed-maturity products.
Political and fiscal paths diverge
The same principle applies to sovereigns, but for different reasons. Here, 
the dividing lines are increasingly political and fiscal. Higher yields have 
improved the income available from European government bonds, while 
diverging fiscal trajectories could create greater differentiation among 
countries. As elections approach, we expect investors to focus increasingly 
on fiscal sustainability, political stability and governments’ capacity to 
deliver reform.
France is perhaps the clearest example. For bond investors, the important 
question is less the election outcome than whether the eventual government 
can deliver credible fiscal consolidation. More broadly, we expect fiscal 
trajectories, growth prospects and political risks to diverge across France, 
Italy and Spain. In this environment, we believe country selection and 
relative value could become increasingly important sources of return 
alongside duration.
Starting yields make the case for income. Dispersion makes the case for 
selectivity. In credit, the dividing lines run across sectors, issuers and capital 
structures; in sovereigns, they are increasingly fiscal and political. We believe 
this growing differentiation favors skilled active management, with security 
selection and relative value offering the potential to capture income while 
seeking additional alpha beyond broad benchmark exposure.

Emerging markets debt (EMD) enters the fourth 
quarter on stronger fundamental footing, with 
resilient growth, healthy external balances and 
yields close to 7%.5 But with spreads in the 
bottom half of the last five years, we see less 
scope for another broad round of compression 
to drive returns. From here, we believe income, 
country selection and potential recovery in core 
rates should matter more.
Policy discipline provides the anchor
Across many emerging markets, policymakers 
have strengthened external resilience, maintained 
restrictive monetary policy to contain inflation 
and continued to consolidate fiscal accounts. 
That discipline is showing up in sovereign credit 
quality: 2026 marks a fourth consecutive year of 
net positive ratings upgrades, led particularly by 
lower-rated countries.6
Recent political shifts, that brought market 
friendly candidates winning in key elections 
(e.g., Hungary, Peru, Colombia, and Brazil) could 
add another source of differentiation, should 
promises of fiscal consolidation and structural 
reforms come to pass.

Over longer horizons, starting yield can be an 
important driver of forward returns. For example, 
income cushion has helped offset the drag from 
higher Treasury yields this year. EM spreads have 
also tightened even as U.S. investment-grade and 
high-yield corporate spreads widened, consistent 
with the relative strength in EM fundamentals.
Flows to EMD have remained resilient despite

to rebuild.7 That shifts the return equation: with 
less room for broad spread compression, starting 
income should provide a larger share of potential 
returns, while dispersion creates opportunities 
to add value through country, currency and 
security selection.

Different markets, different return drivers
The opportunity is far from uniform across emerging markets, with wide 
dispersion across countries, currencies and sectors. This year, returns 
have been driven more by selection than broad index beta, reinforcing the 
importance of differentiating among markets from here.
For hard currency sovereigns, we continue to favor income over duration, with 
a preference for the intermediate part of the curve and a more cautious stance 
toward longer-dated bonds. Within credit, we favor high yield over investment 
grade, where tighter spreads provide less cushion against higher core rates. 
Within high yield, we see the most compelling opportunities in lower-rated 
sovereigns where fiscal adjustment, policy reform or improving fundamentals 
could support further credit-rating upgrades and selective capital appreciation.
Within EM corporates, attractive income with relatively lower duration than 
sovereign debt, supports a favorable income-to-volatility profile. We favor 
BB-rated issuers, where spreads can provide a buffer against global rates 
volatility while avoiding some of the credit risk further down the ratings spectrum. 
At the sector level, we see opportunities in metals and mining, supported by 
higher commodity prices and stronger balance sheets, as well as Latin American 
telecoms, where consolidation and relatively resilient revenues are supportive.
In local currency debt, we remain cautious on broad directional duration as 
higher developed market rates continue to create a challenging backdrop, 
instead favoring bottom-up opportunities in markets offering attractive 
real yields and differentiated monetary-policy. In currencies, we retain a 
constructive medium-term view on EMFX but favor relative value and carry – 
e.g., Colombia 4% and Brazil 7.5% - over broad directional beta dynamics.
The risks to watch
Core duration is the first risk, where the speed of the move may matter more 
than the level. EM credit has absorbed elevated developed-market yields 
when the adjustment has been orderly, but history shows that a 50bps+ move 
within two months leads to EM spreads widening, while otherwise the beta 
from yields to spreads is negative. Second comes oil prices, with the forward 
curve remaining above roughly $85 through mid-2027, higher energy costs 
could slow disinflation, leave central banks less room to cut rates and weigh 
on importers and consumers.8

A wider world of income
14
Emerging anchors for diversification
For years, Japan provided a natural anchor for global bond yields. As 
Japanese rates normalize and long-end pressure returns to developed-
market curves, we see Chinese government bonds emerging as a 
differentiated source of stability.
That contrast was evident last quarter: while U.S. Treasuries were volatile, 
China continued to chart its own path. Chinese government bond yields 
remained near year-to-date lows in August, with 10-year and 30-year yields 
around 1.68% and 2.16%, respectively.10
China’s resilience stems from its distinct economic cycle, subdued 
inflation, ample domestic liquidity and accommodative policy backdrop. 
More broadly, we believe differentiated Asian rate cycles can provide valuable 
balance for global portfolios when the direction of developed-market yields 
remains uncertain.
AI is not just an equity story
AI has captured investors’ attention largely through equities, but building 
the infrastructure behind it will require significant debt capital. Data centers, 
power generation, grid infrastructure and computing capacity are broadening 
the investment opportunity to those financing and enabling AI’s growth.
Asia’s story also differs from the U.S., where concerns have centered on heavy 
issuance from a small group of hyperscalers. Across Asia, financing demand 
spans a more diverse ecosystem of data-center operators, infrastructure 
owners and project financings, led by India and Malaysia, with Australia, 
Indonesia and Japan adding scale.
APAC ex-China data-center capacity is expected to grow from around 20 
gigawatts (GW) today to more than 53 GW by 2030, requiring nearly US$300 
billion of cumulative capital expenditure.11 Around US$95 billion could be 
funded through public bonds, equivalent to approximately US$24 billion 
annually.12 That would represent only about 10% of annual APAC 
investment-grade issuance. We believe this supply could broaden and 
diversify the regional credit universe rather than overwhelm the market.
The bigger opportunity may lie beyond public markets. If public bonds fund 
only US$95 billion of the projected US$300 billion required, a substantial 
financing gap remains. Banks are meeting much of that demand through 
construction loans and project finance, but the scale of the build-out will 
require other sources of long-term capital.
Beyond public markets
That gap is creating opportunities across loans, private credit, infrastructure 
debt and bespoke credit. We are seeing a growing opportunity to structure 
bespoke financing for borrowers whose needs fall between traditional public 
and private markets.
Our recent investments in Skyroot Aerospace and Adani Airports illustrate 
the expertise required to access these markets. Skyroot required an approach 
bridging traditional credit and venture equity, combining lender protections 
with participation in future growth, while Adani Airports required analysis 
of a complex infrastructure platform exposed to India’s long-term growth in 
mobility, consumption and urbanization.
These investments are very different, but the philosophy is consistent: source 
through deep relationships, underwrite what others may find difficult and 
partner with companies as their financing needs evolve.
As we enter the fourth quarter, opportunity and discipline need to go hand in 
hand. Not every company exposed to AI will succeed, and not every structure 
will adequately compensate investors for its complexity. In our view, rigorous 
sourcing, underwriting and security selection will be critical to turning Asia’s 
growing demand for capital into durable investment returns.

For investors, that changes the return equation. Elevated starting yields 
allow more potential return to come from income while providing additional 
cushion against further rate volatility. That is particularly valuable with 
uncertainty still surrounding inflation, Federal Reserve policy and longer-
term interest rates. In our view, investors do not need to correctly predict

repricing offers an opportunity to lock in historically attractive income and

When tax policy changes the credit
The midterm elections could introduce a different source of dispersion. Some 
state and local tax proposals have implications that extend beyond how much 
revenue municipalities collect to how predictable those revenues may be.
Property tax reform, for example, could shift some school funding from 
relatively stable local levies toward more cyclical state income and sales 
taxes. For affected school districts, that could increase budget volatility and 
dependence on state fiscal health, political priorities and the reliability of 
replacement revenues. Credits with protected debt-service levies, permanent 
replacement funding or other safeguards may therefore prove better 
insulated from such changes.
Proposals for one-time taxes on high-net-worth residents raise a different 
question. They could provide substantial near-term revenue, but temporary 
proceeds would not necessarily address structural deficits and could create 
longer-term risks if used to fund permanent programs.
These dynamics reinforce our preference for revenue bonds supported by 
stable cash flows over general obligation credits that may be more exposed 
to changing tax and political conditions. Within investment grade, we see 
opportunities in the transportation, housing and corporate sectors. We also 
favor the 10- to 15-year portion of the curve, where attractive roll-down 
potential, combined with opportunities in 3% and 4% coupon bonds, can 
offer a compelling total return profile.
More income, more reason to be selective
Entering Q4, investors face a compelling combination of attractive yields, 
improved relative value and the potential for a more supportive technical 
backdrop. At the same time, evolving tax and political risks may drive greater 
credit dispersion. We believe this environment favors an active and selective 
approach, capturing relative value while remaining disciplined about where 
and how risk is taken.

As public markets have become more concentrated 
and traditional sources of diversification harder to find, 
investors are looking more broadly for income, long-
term growth and resilience. That is bringing greater 
attention to the role private assets can play alongside 
public markets.
Across both institutional and wealth clients, we hear a 
similar evolution in the conversation. The question is 
no longer simply whether to allocate to private assets, 
but how to use them alongside public markets.
That requires a more deliberate approach to portfolio 
construction. Investors are thinking more carefully 
about where private-market exposures fit, how they 
interact with public holdings and what role each 
exposure is intended to play within the portfolio 
as a whole. For institutions, that can mean 
navigating complexity across managers, vintages and 
strategies. For wealth investors, it means considering 
implementation, suitability, liquidity characteristics 
and time horizons. In both cases, the objective is not 
access for its own sake, but better portfolio outcomes.
At the same time, the financing needs of companies 
and asset owners are becoming larger and more 
complex. The artificial intelligence (AI) buildout is one 
example. It will require substantial capital across 
technology, digital infrastructure, power and related 
assets, often spanning multiple parts of the capital 
structure. That is creating demand for flexible 
financing solutions and long-duration capital at a 
scale that few sources of funding can meet alone.
These shifts are also changing what investors 
expect from their partners. Clients increasingly want 
fewer, more strategic relationships and greater help 
connecting investment opportunities to broader 
portfolio objectives. They need support evaluating 
trade-offs across return, risk and liquidity, 
understanding how exposures interact, and adapting 
portfolios as markets and objectives evolve.
Meeting those needs requires more than a 
broader range of products. It requires investment 
capabilities across public and private markets, deep 
underwriting expertise, portfolio-construction insight, 
technology and data, all brought together around 
the client’s objectives.
That is why BlackRock has been scaling and 
connecting our capabilities across private markets 
while continuing to build on the strength of our 
public-markets platform, technology and portfolio-
construction expertise. Our goal is to help clients 
access opportunities, finance long-term growth and 
build more complete portfolios with greater coherence 
across public and private assets.
The 2027 Private Markets Outlook reflects how we are 
responding to those needs. It examines the structural 
forces reshaping private markets, where opportunities 
are emerging across asset classes and how those 
opportunities can fit within a broader portfolio. Our 
commitment is to bring BlackRock’s capabilities 
together around clients’ objectives as a thoughtful, 
disciplined and long-term partner.

The expansion of private markets reflects a broader shift in 
how economic activity is financed. Capital requirements are 
rising across technology, infrastructure and industry as 
companies and projects become larger, more complex and 
longer term. Companies are also staying private for longer, 
increasing their reliance on private capital to fund growth. At 
the same time, governments face mounting fiscal pressures, 
with global public debt expected to continue rising.¹ Banks 
and public markets remain essential, but no single source of 
capital can meet the economy’s expanding financing needs.
Rather than replacing traditional financing channels, private 
capital is becoming more interconnected with them. Banks, 
public markets and private investors increasingly work 
alongside one another to finance growth. Private credit 
illustrates this relationship. By the second half of 2025, 
private credit represented roughly US$1.4 trillion, or 10%, of 
U.S. nonfinancial corporate debt, while the Federal Reserve 
estimated that private equity, business development 
companies (BDCs) and private credit vehicles accounted for 
approximately 25% of banks’ loan commitments to nonbank 
financial institutions.²

New fund structures, liquidity solutions and data are allowing more investors 
to bring public and private markets together in their portfolios. 
How structural investment needs are 
expanding private-market opportunities
AI is one of the clearest examples of the structural forces 
driving demand for private capital. Deploying AI at scale 
requires substantial investment in data centers, 
semiconductor capacity, power generation, transmission 
and digital connectivity, along with the skilled labor needed 
to build that infrastructure. Global electricity consumption 
from data centers is expected to roughly double from about 
485 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025 to 950 TWh by 2030, 
with data center expansion projected to account for roughly 
half of U.S. electricity-demand growth over that period.³
The AI buildout is creating opportunities across 
infrastructure equity and debt, corporate and asset-backed 
credit, real estate and private equity. As development needs 
grow, AI-related infrastructure developers and owners are 
expected to draw on a mix of equity, bank lending, corporate 
and securitized debt and project finance.⁴
A maturing market reshapes how investors 
manage capital
Private markets enter 2027 with a growing tension between 
investors’ appetite for long-term exposure and the need to 
manage existing assets. Private equity illustrates this 
challenge: 46% of institutional investors surveyed by 
Preqin plan to increase their long-term allocations, even as 
72% identify the exit environment as a major concern.⁵ With 
traditional exit activity remaining constrained, distributions 
have slowed, limiting the capital available for reinvestment. 
According to Bain & Company’s 2026 Global Private Equity 
Report, distributions as a percentage of net asset value 
have remained below 15% for four consecutive years.6 
These dynamics are driving demand for secondaries, 
continuation vehicles and general partner/limited partner 
(GP/LP) solutions that allow investors and managers to 
reshape portfolios rather than rely solely on traditional exits.
These liquidity pressures are also shaping how some 
investors approach private-market allocations. Recent U.S. 
survey evidence suggests many family offices are shifting 
their focus from gaining access to private markets toward 
optimizing existing portfolios. Rather than funding new 
commitments through large reallocations from public 
markets, they expect to rely primarily on distributions from 
existing private-market investments and cash on hand.7 
Nearly half (49%) intend to maintain their current 
allocations over the next 12 months, while roughly 
equal numbers expect to increase (17%) or decrease 
(15%) allocations.8 This shifts liquidity from a tactical 
consideration to a portfolio construction challenge, linking 
commitment pacing, cash-flow forecasting and portfolio 
rebalancing more closely than in the past. 
Investors are also scrutinizing the underlying drivers 
of performance more closely. Private credit conditions 
remained broadly healthy through the second quarter of 
2026, but performance became more differentiated as 
weaker and more highly leveraged borrowers came under 
greater scrutiny.9 This highlights the importance of 
underwriting discipline, sector selection and capital 
structure positioning.
As private markets become a larger part of portfolios, 
investors need greater visibility into risk exposures, cash 
flows, valuations and manager performance across public 
and private holdings. Advances in data, benchmarking and 
portfolio analytics are improving transparency, making the 
ability to monitor and manage private assets nearly as 
important as the ability to access them.
How a broader investor base is changing 
private markets
As private markets become a more established part of 
portfolio construction, the investor base is expanding with 
them. Institutions remain the foundation, but wealth 
investors are playing a larger role as model portfolios, 
evergreen funds and other structures make private assets 
more accessible. As participation expands, investors 
need a clearer understanding of liquidity, risk, suitability
and return expectations.
Institutions and wealth investors approach these 
challenges from different starting points. Established 
institutional programs focus more on managing existing 
exposures through commitment pacing, stress testing, 
secondaries and integrated GP/LP solutions. Wealth 
investors, meanwhile, need to consider how private 
assets fit within their broader portfolios, particularly their 
liquidity needs and investment horizons. Newer family 
offices express particular concern about illiquidity 
and transparency.10

Nearly a third of ultra-high-net-worth investors already 
allocate to private markets, although performance 
uncertainty, fees and complexity remain meaningful 
barriers to broader adoption. Most surveyed investors said 
they would accept some reduction in liquidity in exchange 
for higher expected returns.11 As access expands, these 
expectations for transparency, reporting and portfolio

Why investment selection matters as 
private markets grow
Investor conviction is becoming more differentiated as 
private markets expand. According to Preqin Investor 
Surveys, infrastructure stands out, with 47% of investors 
planning to increase long-term allocations, followed by 
private equity at 46% and hedge funds at 36%. Demand for 
power and digital infrastructure to support AI-related 
growth is helping drive interest.
Strong demand, however, does not guarantee attractive 
returns. Competition for assets, construction and 
operational challenges, technological disruption and 
financing risk can erode returns. In our view, disciplined 
underwriting, asset selection and execution will 
increasingly distinguish outcomes.
Credit markets illustrate the importance of selectivity. 
In February 2026, broadly syndicated software loans fell 
4.17%, compared with a 0.78% decline for the overall 
leveraged-loan index, as investors reassessed the 
implications of AI disruption.12 Performance increasingly 
reflects differences in business quality, leverage and cash-
flow resilience, underscoring the distinction between 
lending against projected business growth and lending 
against contracted or asset-backed cash flows. 
AI may create substantial financing demand, but not 
every data center, power asset, borrower or financing 
structure will be equally attractive. Entry valuation, 
contractual revenues, counterparties, capital structure 
and the durability of underlying demand will help determine 
outcomes, alongside the ability to improve operational 
and commercial performance. 
What private-market growth means for 
investors in 2027
As private capital plays a larger role in financing economic 
growth, disciplined underwriting, investment selection and 
portfolio construction are likely to matter even more. The 
sections that follow examine where we see opportunities, 
the risks investors should consider and how private assets 
can contribute to broader portfolio objectives.

Structural trends shaping infrastructure 
investment 
An estimated US$106 trillion of global infrastructure 
investments across new projects and the modernization of 
existing assets are required over the next 15 years.13 Among 
the key structural forces shaping these requirements are 
exponential growth in AI, further digitalization, evolving 
energy needs, aging infrastructure and economic 
development in emerging markets. 
AI and digital infrastructure
The rapid adoption of AI is accelerating demand for computing 
capacity, with global data center demand projected to grow at 
approximately 20% annually through 2030, requiring an 
estimated US$1.5 trillion in investment. 14 This build-out 
broadens the investment requirements into associated power 
infrastructure in power generation and transmission. 
At the same time, broader digitalization of the global economy 
expands data consumption and cloud computing, highlighting 
the need to bolster communications networks. Accommodating 
this growth in data consumption will require continued build-
out of fiber networks, telecommunications towers and wireless 
infrastructure, including upgrades to 5G and eventually 6G. 
Energy infrastructure
Beyond the needs of the digital economy, rising electricity 
consumption, energy security considerations and 
decarbonization objectives are reshaping investment 
priorities across the power and energy sectors. Global energy-
transition investment reached a record US$2.3 trillion in 
2025, with average annual spending projected to reach 
US$3.1 trillion between 2026 and 2030.15 Meeting these 
evolving needs will require a comprehensive set of solutions 
spanning renewable and conventional power generation, 
energy storage and upgrades to electricity networks.
Aging infrastructure and growth in 
emerging markets
In developed economies, infrastructure built during earlier 
periods of economic expansion are increasingly overdue for 
maintenance, replacement and modernization. 
Transportation systems, energy networks and utilities are 
facing substantial capital requirements to maintain service 
quality and accommodate growing needs. In the U.S. alone, 
the American Society of Civil Engineers estimates US$9.1 
trillion of infrastructure investment needs through 2033.16 
Population growth, urbanization and rising economic activity 
are increasing the need for new infrastructure across 
developing economies. Expanding electricity access, 
transportation networks and digital connectivity will be 
essential to accommodate growing populations, support 
industrial development and improve living standards.

Transportation infrastructure represents another key driver of 
demand for infrastructure investments, with McKinsey 
estimating US$36 trillion of required funding by 2040.17 
Sustained long-term growth in global aviation demand, 
driven primarily by rising incomes and the expansion of the 
middle class, creates a structural need for growth in aviation 
infrastructure. Ports are also attracting substantial capital 
investment, as changing trade patterns and supply chains 
require expanded cargo-handling capacity as well as 
construction of and upgrades to terminals.
In focus: A new era of electricity demand
Among the most important building blocks of this 
accelerated need for infrastructure investments is growth in 
power demand. Between 2026 and 2030, the International 
Energy Agency projects global electricity consumption to 
grow by an average of 3.6% annually, around 50% faster 
than the preceding decade.¹⁷ Another notable shift is the 
resurgence of electricity use in advanced economies, where 
growth had largely flatlined for the past 15 years. In the U.S., 
for example, momentum in data center development is 
projected to push electricity consumption higher at nearly 
twice the rate of the past decade.
Meeting these needs will require investment across the entire 
power system, from generation and natural gas infrastructure 
to transmission, distribution, storage and grid modernization. 
The challenge is not producing more electricity, but delivering 
reliable and affordable power where and when it is  needed.
Beyond physical assets: Strategic priorities 
for governments and corporates 
Today, infrastructure’s importance extends beyond its role as 
the physical backbone of the global economy: It is a strategic 
priority for governments and corporates alike. Amid 
geopolitical uncertainty and economic fragmentation, energy 
security, resilient supply chains, and domestic industrial 
capacity are central to economic stability and 
competitiveness. The race for AI leadership adds further 
urgency, as technological ambitions are deeply intertwined 
with access to reliable power, digital connectivity and the 
ability to add infrastructure capacity at scale.
Meeting the demand: Structural constraints 
on supply 
The growing need for infrastructure investment comes at a 
time when traditional sources of capital are increasingly 
constrained. Governments are contending with mounting 
deficits, elevated debt burdens and competing fiscal 
priorities. Across Organisation for Economic Co-operation 
and Development (OECD) countries, government gross debt 
exceeded 110% of GDP in 2025 and is projected to continue 
this trajectory, placing further pressure on public finances. 19 
Corporates today also face the challenge of competing capex 
priorities and balance sheet constraints in navigating the 
capital-intensive nature of infrastructure 
investments. Public markets, while an important source of 
capital, also present the challenge of uneven capital 
formation and increasing competition, limiting their ability to 
provide long-term, flexible capital solutions. 
Meeting the infrastructure investment demand therefore will 
require expanding the pool of available capital rather than 
simply reallocating existing resources. As this demand for 
capital continues to grow across the infrastructure 
opportunity set, even greater participation from global 
investors than today and an expanded range of capital 
solutions will be critical. 
Private capital: Bridging the infrastructure 
funding gap 
The widening gap between infrastructure investment needs 
and traditional funding capacity is creating an expanding role 
for private markets. Private capital can provide scaled, long-
term capital which, combined with operating expertise, is a 
tailor-made solution to address the complexity of 
infrastructure investments. Strategic partnerships with 
corporates as well as governments represent attractive 
avenues for mobilizing private capital in undertaking new 
infrastructure projects. Furthermore, take-privates and other 
asset carve-outs also present opportunity to unlock 
additional growth and expansion of existing infrastructure. 
We believe this primacy of private capital in infrastructure 
represents a natural evolution of capital solutions to meet the 
foundational need for investing in the world’s future.
Private infrastructure: Ballast in a 
diversified portfolio 
We see private infrastructure’s role in portfolios as evolving 
from a specialized allocation toward a foundational private 
markets ballast. Real infrastructure assets are characterized 
by long-asset lives, high barriers to entry, and contracted 
revenues that can be resilient through economic cycles. 
Across capital structures (equity and credit) and risk 
spectrum, private infrastructure presents investors the 
opportunity to capture attractive income yield, inflation 
protection, and growth upside that is further bolstered by 
long-term structural tailwinds. 
This evolution of private infrastructure is evident in the 
growing share of investor portfolios allocated to 
infrastructure assets. Global infrastructure fundraising 
reached a record of nearly US$200 billion in 2025, while 51% 
of surveyed institutional investors plan to increase their 
infrastructure allocations over the next three years.20 This 
expanding pool of private capital can provide an additional 
source of long-term financing to support infrastructure 
development across sectors and geographies. 
While the opportunity set is large, we believe constructing 
enduring private infrastructure exposures requires discipline, 
expertise and experience: discipline in delivering 
differentiated, relationship-driven investments, expertise in 
executing concrete operational improvements, and 
experience in consistently monetizing the value created 
through well-timed exits.

Structured corporate investments combine elements of 
traditional corporate lending and asset-based finance. 
The issuer may be a bankruptcy-remote SPV or joint venture 
established to hold a defined pool of assets that serves as 
collateral to the structured corporate debt, and the credit 
agreement may include credit support, such as loan-to-value 
(LTV)-based rapid amortization triggers, that are typically 
found in ABF transactions.
Lenders may also benefit from credit enhancement provided 
by a high-quality, often investment-grade-rated corporate 
operating company (the “parent”). The credit enhancement 
can take different forms, such as an explicit parent guarantee 
(the most straightforward structure) or a call or put structure 
that has the effect of collapsing the SPV and folding 
lenders into the parent’s capital structure (a more complex 
approach). Through these features, the structured corporate 
opportunity is evaluated from both an ABF and corporate 
credit perspective, as the credit risk of the parent is often 
the final determinant of debt performance.
Structured corporate transactions can offer attractive 
gross yield premiums relative to a parent company’s 
publicly traded senior unsecured debt. In some cases, 
we believe the structured debt effectively ranks alongside 
the parent’s existing senior unsecured obligations. The 
premium, however, does not necessarily represent an 
arbitrage opportunity.
Rather, structured corporate investors are earning their 
potential yield premium for risk factors other than 
corporate credit risk.
First, the effort required to source, structure and negotiate 
these transactions is substantial. There is significantly more 
capital available to purchase the parent’s public debt than 
to execute such a private transaction, resulting in favorable 
supply and demand dynamics.
Second, the connection between the structured corporate 
debt and the parent can be complex. While explicit 
guarantees provide a direct connection, other provisions, 
such as a lender put right, whereby all equity ownership of 
the SPV is sold to the parent to collapse the SPV structure 
(resulting in structured corporate lenders becoming senior 
unsecured creditors to the parent), require careful legal 
and technical evaluation. 
Finally, these transactions are often executed bilaterally 
(or through a few sophisticated lenders) and therefore 
exhibit limited secondary liquidity. Investors do not have 
the ability to de-risk their position through a sale in the 
secondary market, and so they are compensated with an 
illiquidity premium.
Structured corporate financings illustrate how public and 
private credit markets are becoming more interconnected. 
When the borrower’s goal is to minimize cost of capital, 
public markets typically lead to best execution. However, 
when the objective is to preserve public debt capacity, solve 
a specific financing need or raise bespoke capital, private 
markets may offer an attractive solution.

Scarcity converts demand into pricing power
Strong demand alone does not make a good investment. The 
critical question is whether supply can respond, and how 
quickly. Some constraints are cyclical: Development may 
restart as financing conditions improve. Other limitations are 
structural and harder to overcome, such as land availability, 
planning, secured power and grid capacity. 
Higher construction and financing costs have already 
reduced development pipelines across many property types. 
Yet the most durable opportunities may lie where scarcity 
persists even after capital markets recover: sites that cannot 
easily be replicated, locations where planning takes years or 
assets requiring specialist operating capabilities.
The result is scarcity within abundance. There can be too 
much real estate overall, yet too little of the space tenants 
actually want. An extensive data center pipeline is not the 
same as deliverable capacity. Abundant industrial space does 
not guarantee suitable logistics facilities close to consumers. 
Strong housing need does not ensure homes can be 
delivered at affordable rents.

formation, changing industrial networks and digital demand 
with constrained supply. The opportunity is not simply to buy 
into a regional recovery, but to address specific shortages in 
living, logistics and data centers.

Dispersion is the opportunity
As private markets expand, the broad market tailwinds that 
supported returns in the previous cycle are fading, creating 
greater dispersion across asset classes, managers and 
individual investments. Capturing these opportunities 
while managing the associated risks requires a more 
selective approach to portfolio construction, with a clear 
understanding of the underlying drivers of portfolio returns.
Selectivity across the opportunity set
Infrastructure remains a high-conviction asset class. It sits 
at the intersection of digitalization and AI, electrification, 
energy security and demographic change, and is 
characterized by stable counterparties and diversified 
sources of return. AI is not only a software story; it is also a 
power, fiber and grid investment story. We see opportunities 
across digital infrastructure, power generation and energy-
related assets, but we believe entry valuation, leverage and 
execution make selectivity critical. 
In private credit, a more mature and competitive market 
increasingly rewards underwriting discipline, collateral 
quality, structure and complexity. Higher base rates and 
spreads that have widened from their lows following retail 
investor concerns around U.S. BDCs in late 2025 and early 
2026 have created opportunities for selective capital 
deployment. As performance diverges between borrowers, 
we favor higher-quality and more structured opportunities, 
including larger-scale borrowers, asset-backed finance and 
infrastructure debt. 
In private equity, the traditional buyout market continues to 
work through investments made during the era of low 
interest rates, with approximately US$3.3 trillion of 
unrealized value42 across 33,575 unsold PE-backed 
companies globally.43 This has helped transform 
secondaries from a niche liquidity solution into a core 
portfolio construction tool, offering access to seasoned 
assets and managers while mitigating the J-curve effect. 
Growth equity and venture look very different, having 
evolved to become the largest components of the private 
equity market, based on AUM. AI and technological 
disruption are driving significant value creation, but in a 
concentrated manner, highlighting the importance of 
access, valuation discipline and selectivity. 
A relatively small group of late-stage private companies 
has generated an outsized share of recent valuation 
growth. This creates a familiar portfolio challenge: Too little 
exposure to the companies driving market returns can 
create a meaningful performance gap, while too much can 
leave portfolios concentrated in the same companies, 
themes and economic risks. 
A more fragmented market backdrop is reinforcing the case for combining multiple alternative asset 
classes to access a broader range of return drivers. 
Portfolio resilience may come less from traditional diversification labels and more from understanding 
how assets respond to different economic and market conditions. 
The increasing specialization of private markets is creating opportunities for investors to build portfolios 
around specific themes, risks and objectives rather than relying solely on broad asset-class allocations.

Turning dispersion into portfolio outcomes
For investors, effective portfolio construction starts with 
understanding existing exposures, economic risks, liquidity 
needs, return objectives and investment horizons. Rather 
than relying on predetermined asset-class allocations, 
investors can evaluate opportunities across primaries, 
secondaries, co-investments and direct investments, while

more on what investors own, the price they pay and how 
investments are structured and implemented.
Diversification beyond asset-class labels
Allocating across private equity, credit, infrastructure 
and real estate does not necessarily diversify economic 
risk. Different asset classes can share exposure to growth, 
equity markets, credit spreads, interest rates and 
refinancing conditions.
Investors can use private-market data, benchmarks and 
portfolio analytics to identify overlapping exposures, assess 
relative value and evaluate how individual investments 
contribute to overall portfolio risk. We also see opportunities 
in private-market diversifiers with returns driven more 
by asset-specific factors, including intellectual property 
and royalties, insurance and risk transfer, and other 
nontraditional real assets.
These investments can complement core private-market 
exposures with differentiated cash flows that are less 
dependent on traditional corporate earnings or market 
beta. Diversification is not simply about owning more 
asset classes. It is about owning genuinely different risks.
